    Section 2.7 Triple Integrals in Spherical Coordinates

    Today, we will study yet another useful coordinate system in three dimensions, which is known as the spherical coordinate system. It simplifies the evaluation of triple integrals over regions bounded by spheres or cones.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.1 Spherical Coordinates

    The spherical coordinates can be best explained in a figure. Consider the point \(P\) with spherical coordinates \((\rho,\theta,\phi)\) shown in Figure 2.7.1.
    🔗
    1. \(\rho\) is the distance of the point \(P\) from the origin (\(\rho \ge 0\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\theta\) is the angle the projection of \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) makes with the positive \(x\)-axis. This is the same \(\theta\) as in cylindrical coordinates, (2.6.1) (\(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\phi\) is the angle between \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) and the positive \(z\)-axis (\(0 \le \phi \le \pi\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Equations Relating Spherical Coordinates to Cartesian and Cylindrical Coordinates.

    \begin{align} r \amp = \rho\sin\phi,\tag{2.7.1}\\ z \amp = \rho\cos\phi,\notag\\ x \amp = r\cos\theta\notag\\ \amp = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\notag\\ y \amp = r\sin\theta\notag\\ \amp = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\notag\\ \rho \amp = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\notag\\ \amp = \sqrt{r^2 + z^2}.\notag \end{align}
    🔗
    🔗
    Spheres and cones have particularly simple equations in spherical coordinates. For a sphere centered at the origin with radius \(R\text{,}\) we have
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = R^2\\ \amp \Rightarrow (\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta)^2 + (\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta)^2\\ \amp \qquad + (\rho\cos\phi)^2 = R^2\\ \amp \Rightarrow \rho^2\left(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta\right)\sin^2\phi\\ \amp \qquad + \rho^2\cos^2\phi = R^2\\ \amp \Rightarrow \rho^2\sin^2\phi + \rho^2\cos^2\phi = R^2\\ \amp \Rightarrow \rho^2 = R^2 \Rightarrow \rho = R. \end{align*}

    Example 2.7.2. Example I.

    Find the equation of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + (z-1)^2 = 1\) in spherical coordinates.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    Substituting the spherical expressions (2.7.1) for \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(z\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp x^2 + y^2 + (z-1)^2 = 1\\ \amp \Rightarrow (\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta)^2 + (\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta)^2\\ \amp \qquad + (\rho\cos\phi - 1)^2 = 1\\ \amp \Rightarrow \rho^2\left(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta\right)\sin^2\phi\\ \amp \qquad + \rho^2\cos^2\phi - 2\rho\cos\phi + 1 = 1\\ \amp \Rightarrow \rho^2\sin^2\phi + \rho^2\cos^2\phi\\ \amp \qquad - 2\rho\cos\phi = 0\\ \amp \Rightarrow \rho^2 = 2\rho\cos\phi \Rightarrow \rho = 2\cos\phi. \end{align*}
    🔗

    Example 2.7.3. Example II.

    What are the equations of the two cones \(z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) and \(z = -\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) in spherical coordinates?
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    The upper cone makes a \(45^\circ\) angle with the positive \(z\)-axis, so every point on it has \(\phi = \pi/4\text{;}\) likewise the lower cone is \(\phi = 3\pi/4\text{.}\) (Algebraically: \(\rho\cos\phi = \rho\sin\phi\) forces \(\tan\phi = 1\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗

    Coordinate Conversion Formulas.

    \begin{align*} \amp \text{Cylindrical to rectangular:}\\ \amp \quad x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta, \quad z = z\\ \amp \text{Spherical to rectangular:}\\ \amp \quad x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta, \quad y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\\ \amp \quad z = \rho\cos\phi\\ \amp \text{Spherical to cylindrical:}\\ \amp \quad r = \rho\sin\phi, \quad z = \rho\cos\phi, \quad \theta = \theta \end{align*}
    The corresponding formulas for \(dV\) in triple integrals (see (2.6.2) and (2.7.2)):
    \begin{align*} dV \amp = dx\,dy\,dz = dz\ r\,dr\,d\theta\\ \amp = \rho^2\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.2 Integration in Spherical Coordinates

    The volume element \(dV\) in spherical coordinates is illustrated in Figure 2.7.4: a spherical wedge with radial side \(d\rho\text{,}\) one arc side \(\rho\,d\phi\text{,}\) and the other arc side \(\rho\sin\phi\,d\theta\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{equation} dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.\tag{2.7.2} \end{equation}
    Then, the triple integral over a region \(D\) described in spherical coordinates by \(\alpha\le\theta\le\beta\text{,}\) \(\phi_{\min}\le\phi\le\phi_{\max}\text{,}\) and \(g_1(\phi,\theta)\le\rho\le g_2(\phi,\theta)\) can be converted into spherical coordinates as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{align} \amp \iiint_D f(x,y,z)\,dV\notag\\ \amp \quad = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\!\!\int_{\phi_{\min}}^{\phi_{\max}}\!\!\int_{g_1(\theta,\phi)}^{g_2(\theta,\phi)}\notag\\ \amp \qquad f\big(\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\notag\\ \amp \qquad \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\ \rho\cos\phi\big)\notag\\ \amp \qquad \rho^2\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.\tag{2.7.3} \end{align}
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.3 The Ice Cream Cone

    Example 2.7.5. Example III.

    Find the volume of the “ice cream cone” \(D\) cut from the solid sphere \(\rho \le 1\) by the cone \(\phi = \pi/3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We begin by plotting the ice cream cone (Figure 2.7.6). Next, we identify the region of integration, which will provide us with the integration limits:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} D = \{(\rho,\theta,\phi) : \amp 0\le\theta\le 2\pi,\\ \amp 0\le\phi\le\frac{\pi}{3},\\ \amp 0\le\rho\le 1\}. \end{align*}
    Thus, by (2.5.2) and (2.7.3), the volume of the ice cream cone can be evaluated as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} V \amp= \iiint_D \rho^2\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/3}\!\!\int_0^1 \rho^2\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/3} \left[\frac{\rho^3}{3}\right]_0^1 \sin\phi\ d\phi\,d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/3} \frac13\sin\phi\ d\phi\,d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\left[-\frac13\cos\phi\right]_0^{\pi/3} d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\left(-\frac16 + \frac13\right)d\theta\\ \amp= \frac16(2\pi) = \frac{\pi}{3}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The limits of integration can be read as three nested sweeps, and Figure 2.7.7 builds \(D\) from them, innermost first.
    🔗
    How the spherical description of the ice cream cone works—the angle \(\phi\) sweeping from the \(z\)-axis down to the cone while \(\rho\) reaches out to the sphere—is animated in Figure 2.7.8.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.4 Converting a Triple Integral to Spherical Coordinates

    Example 2.7.9. Example IV.

    Calculate the triple integral below by converting it to spherical coordinates:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_{-2}^{2}\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{4-x^2}}^{\sqrt{4-x^2}} \!\!\int_{2-\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}}^{2+\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}}\\ \amp \qquad \left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2} dz\,dy\,dx. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    We begin by converting the integration limits and the integrand to spherical coordinates. The limits describe the solid sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + (z-2)^2 = 4\) of radius \(2\) centered at \((0,0,2)\text{,}\) shown in Figure 2.7.10:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp z = 2 \pm\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}\\ \amp \Rightarrow \rho\cos\phi = 2 \pm\sqrt{4 - \rho^2\sin^2\phi}\\ \amp \Rightarrow (\rho\cos\phi - 2)^2 = 4 - \rho^2\sin^2\phi\\ \amp \Rightarrow \rho^2 = 4\rho\cos\phi \Rightarrow \rho = 4\cos\phi. \end{align*}
    Note that \(z = 2+\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}\) is the upper hemisphere and \(z = 2-\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}\) is the lower hemisphere. In spherical coordinates the solid is \(0\le\rho\le 4\cos\phi\text{,}\) \(0\le\phi\le\frac{\pi}{2}\text{,}\) \(0\le\theta\le 2\pi\text{,}\) and the integrand is \(\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2} = \left(\rho^2\right)^{3/2} = \rho^3\text{.}\) Thus (2.7.3) gives:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{4\cos\phi}\\ \amp \qquad \left(\rho^2\right)^{3/2}\rho^2\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\\ \amp = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{4\cos\phi} \rho^5\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\\ \amp = \frac{4^6}{6}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^6\phi\,\sin\phi\ d\phi\,d\theta\\ \amp = -\frac{4^6}{42}\int_0^{2\pi} \Big[\cos^7\phi\Big]_0^{\pi/2}\,d\theta\\ \amp = \frac{4096\pi}{21}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.5 Re-Writing an Integral in Spherical and Cylindrical Coordinates

    Example 2.7.11. Example V.

    Re-write (do not evaluate) the following integral in spherical and cylindrical coordinates:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-1}^{1}\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{1-x^2}}^{0} \!\!\int_{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}^{1} (x^2+y^2)\, dz\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The \(xy\)-projection is the lower half disk \(y \le 0\text{,}\) \(x^2+y^2\le 1\) (shown in Figure 2.7.12), so \(\pi \le \theta \le 2\pi\) and \(0 \le r \le 1\text{.}\) In \(z\text{,}\) the solid runs from the cone \(z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) (that is, \(z = r\)) up to the plane \(z = 1\text{;}\) the solid is shown in Figure 2.7.13. Since \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\text{,}\) the cylindrical form (2.6.3) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_r^1 r^2\, r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta. \end{equation*}
    For the spherical form, the cone is \(\phi = \pi/4\text{,}\) and the plane \(z = 1\) becomes \(\rho\cos\phi = 1\text{,}\) that is, \(\rho = \sec\phi\text{.}\) With \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2 = \rho^2\sin^2\phi\text{,}\) (2.7.3) gives
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/4}\!\!\int_0^{\sec\phi}\\ \amp \qquad \left(\rho^2\sin^2\phi\right)\rho^2\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Figure 2.7.14 and Figure 2.7.15 build the solid from each set of limits in turn, innermost first: the two descriptions fill the same flat triangle in different ways before turning it about the \(z\)-axis.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.7.16.

    Calculate the volume of the region inside \(\rho = 2\cos\phi\) and below \(z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\text{.}\)
