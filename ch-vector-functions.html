    Section 1.1 Arc Length of a Curve

    When an object moves along a curve, we often want to know the total distance it travels. In this section we build the arc-length integral, first in the plane and then in space, and we apply it to two motions: a point sweeping out a quarter circle and a paraglider spiralling to the ground. The key idea throughout is simple: distance travelled is speed accumulated over time.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.1 Arc Length in 2D

    Imagine that at time \(t=a\) an object starts moving from an initial point \(A\) and at time \(t=b\) it reaches the final point \(B\text{.}\) During this period the object traces out a smooth curve \(C\) in the plane, as shown in Figure 1.1. How can we calculate the distance the object has travelled?
    🔗
    The idea is to measure the curve one tiny piece at a time. Over a very short time interval the object moves a small amount \(dx\) horizontally and \(dy\) vertically, so by the Pythagorean theorem it covers a tiny arc of length
    \begin{equation*} ds=\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2}. \end{equation*}
    Adding up all these tiny lengths from \(A\) to \(B\)—that is, integrating—gives the total length of the curve:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_A^B ds = \int_A^B \sqrt{dx^2+dy^2}. \end{equation*}
    To turn this into a formula we can compute, suppose the curve \(C\) is parametrized as \((x(t),y(t))\) for \(a\le t\le b\text{,}\) or equivalently in vector form \(\mathbf{r}(t)=x(t)\,\mathbf{i}+y(t)\,\mathbf{j}\text{.}\) Factoring \(dt\) out of the square root converts the integral into an ordinary integral over time:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_A^B ds = \int_a^b \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt = \int_a^b \left|\mathbf{v}(t)\right|\,dt, \end{equation*}
    where in the last step we recognized the velocity vector
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}(t)=\frac{d\mathbf{r}(t)}{dt} =\left(\frac{dx(t)}{dt},\frac{dy(t)}{dt}\right), \end{equation*}
    whose length is the object’s speed:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left|\mathbf{v}(t)\right| =\sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}. \end{equation*}
    In words: arc length is the integral of speed over time. This is the natural generalization of the familiar rule “distance equals speed times time” to motion whose speed and direction change from moment to moment.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2. Length of a quarter circle.

    Using the arc-length formula in 2D, calculate the length of a quarter circle of radius one. (We already know the answer: a full unit circle has circumference \(2\pi\text{,}\) so a quarter of it has length \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\text{.}\) The point of this example is to see the formula reproduce it.)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can parametrize the quarter circle as
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t)=(\cos t,\sin t),\qquad 0\le t\le\frac{\pi}{2}. \end{equation*}
    Differentiating, the velocity vector and its length are
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{v}(t) \amp = (-\sin t,\ \cos t),\\ \left|\mathbf{v}(t)\right| \amp = \sqrt{\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t}=1. \end{align*}
    The speed is constant and equal to \(1\text{,}\) so the arc length is simply the elapsed time:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_C ds = \int_0^{\pi/2}\left|\mathbf{v}(t)\right|\,dt = \int_0^{\pi/2} 1\,dt = \frac{\pi}{2}, \end{equation*}
    as expected. Notice a useful by-product: because the speed is \(1\text{,}\) the arc length swept out up to time \(t\) is \(s(t)=t\) exactly. The motion is shown in Figure 1.3, the position and velocity vectors in Figure 1.4, and a snapshot of the partially swept arc in Figure 1.5.

    Subsection 1.1.2 Arc Length in 3D

    The formulas above generalize to motion in space with no new ideas: the tiny arc simply picks up a third term, \(ds=\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2+dz^2}\text{.}\) For a space curve \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\bigl(x(t),y(t),z(t)\bigr)\) we therefore have
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \int_A^B ds \amp = \int_a^b \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt = \int_a^b \left|\mathbf{v}(t)\right|\,dt,\\ \mathbf{v}(t) \amp = \frac{d\mathbf{r}(t)}{dt} = \left(\frac{dx(t)}{dt},\frac{dy(t)}{dt},\frac{dz(t)}{dt}\right),\\ \left|\mathbf{v}(t)\right| \amp = \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)^2}. \end{align*}
    As in the plane, arc length is still the integral of speed over time.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.6. Distance travelled by a paraglider.

    The position of a paraglider at time \(t\) is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t)=\left(t\cos t,\ t\sin t,\ 9\sqrt{8}-\frac{\sqrt{8}}{3}\,t^{3/2}\right). \end{equation*}
    Assuming that the paraglider begins at time \(t=0\text{:}\)
    🔗
    1. How long does it take to reach the ground, that is, \(z=0\text{?}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Calculate the velocity vector at time \(t\text{,}\) and also calculate its length.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Calculate the distance travelled by the paraglider.
      🔗
      🔗
    Solution.
    Part A. The paraglider reaches the ground when the \(z\)-coordinate vanishes:
    \begin{equation*} 9\sqrt{8}-\frac{\sqrt{8}}{3}\,t^{3/2}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t^{3/2}=27 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t=27^{2/3}=9. \end{equation*}
    So it takes \(9\) units of time to land.
    🔗
    Part B. We differentiate each component. The first two require the product rule, and for the third, \(\deriv{}{t}\left(-\frac{\sqrt{8}}{3}t^{3/2}\right) =-\frac{\sqrt{8}}{3}\cdot\frac{3}{2}\,t^{1/2} =-\frac{\sqrt{8}}{2}\,t^{1/2}=-\sqrt{2}\,t^{1/2}\text{.}\) Hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}(t)=\frac{d\mathbf{r}(t)}{dt} =\Bigl(\cos t-t\sin t,\ \sin t+t\cos t,\ -\sqrt{2}\,t^{1/2}\Bigr). \end{equation*}
    Its length simplifies neatly: the cross terms \(\pm 2t\sin t\cos t\) cancel, the squared sines and cosines combine, and the last component contributes \((-\sqrt{2}\,t^{1/2})^2=2t\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \left|\mathbf{v}(t)\right| \amp = \sqrt{(\cos t-t\sin t)^2+(\sin t+t\cos t)^2+2t}\\ \amp = \sqrt{\cos^2 t+t^2\sin^2 t-2t\sin t\cos t + \sin^2 t+t^2\cos^2 t+2t\sin t\cos t+2t}\\ \amp = \sqrt{t^2+2t+1}=\sqrt{(t+1)^2}=t+1, \end{align*}
    where the last equality uses \(t+1>0\text{.}\) So the paraglider’s speed grows linearly: it starts at \(1\) and reaches \(10\) at touchdown.
    🔗
    Part C. The distance travelled is the arc length of the path, that is, speed integrated over the \(9\) units of flight time:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} s=\int_0^{9}\left|\mathbf{v}(t)\right|\,dt =\int_0^{9}(t+1)\,dt =\left[\frac{t^2}{2}+t\right]_0^{9} =\frac{81}{2}+9=49.5. \end{equation*}
    The descending spiral, together with its endpoints \(\langle 0,0,9\sqrt{8}\rangle\) and \(\langle 9\cos 9,\,9\sin 9,\,0\rangle\text{,}\) is animated in Figure 1.7 and shown in Figure 1.8.

    Section 1.2 Line Integrals

    In this section we study line integrals of scalar functions. We develop the idea through a concrete problem: finding the mass of a curved wire when we know its density—its mass per unit length—at every point. The strategy is the one behind every integral: chop the wire into tiny pieces, multiply the density of each piece by its length, and add up the results.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.1 Line Integrals of Scalar Functions

    Suppose we would like to compute the mass of a wire that does not have a trivial shape (such as a straight line). If the wire were straight and its density constant, the mass would simply be density times length. For a curved wire whose density \(\rho(x,y,z)\) varies from point to point, neither shortcut is available—but we can recover the same simple rule on small pieces.
    🔗
    Assume the wire is represented by a curve \(C\) in space, parametrized by \(t\in[a,b]\text{,}\) as in Figure 1.1. Divide the wire into \(n\) small portions, and denote the length of the \(k\)th portion by \(\Delta s_k\text{.}\) Pick a sample point \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\) on this portion. If the portion is short, the density is nearly constant along it, so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{mass of the } k\text{th portion} \approx \rho\left(x_k,y_k,z_k\right)\,\Delta s_k, \end{equation*}
    and summing over all portions,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{mass of the wire} \approx \sum_{k=1}^{n} \rho\left(x_k,y_k,z_k\right)\,\Delta s_k. \end{equation*}
    This is only an approximation, because the density is not actually constant over each portion. How can we make the calculation exact? We take the limit in which every portion becomes infinitely small, that is, \(\Delta s_k\to 0\text{,}\) or equivalently \(n\to\infty\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{mass of the wire} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n} \rho\left(x_k,y_k,z_k\right)\,\Delta s_k. \end{equation*}
    This limit is denoted by the integral
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{mass of the wire} = \int_C \rho\left(x,y,z\right)\,ds, \end{equation*}
    read “the integral of \(\rho\) over the curve \(C\text{.}\)” The process is animated in Figure 1.2: as the partition refines, the sums settle down to the value of the integral.
    🔗
    Our final task is to write the line element \(ds\) in terms of \(dt\text{,}\) so the integral becomes an ordinary integral we can evaluate. Exactly as in Section 1.1, the line element is the length of an infinitesimal displacement in \(\R^3\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} ds=\sqrt{(dx)^2+(dy)^2+(dz)^2}, \end{equation*}
    and factoring out \(dt\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \text{mass of the wire} \amp = \int_C \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right)\,\frac{ds}{dt}\,dt\\ \amp = \int_C \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt. \end{align*}
    The square root is exactly the length of the velocity vector
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}(t)=\mathbf{r}'(t) =\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)\mathbf{i} +\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)\mathbf{j} +\left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)\mathbf{k} =\left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\frac{dy}{dt},\frac{dz}{dt}\right\rangle, \end{equation*}
    so the formula takes the tidy form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{mass of the wire} = \int_a^b \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\right\rVert\,dt. \end{equation*}
    There is nothing special about density here. In general, a scalar line integral has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_C f\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right)\,ds = \int_a^b f\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\right\rVert\,dt, \end{equation*}
    where \(f\) is a scalar-valued function and the curve \(C\) specifies the region of integration. Once the curve is parametrized in terms of \(t\text{,}\) the region of integration translates to \(a\le t\le b\text{.}\) In words: to compute a line integral, trade the geometric element \(ds\) for the parameter element \(\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\,dt\). Two sanity checks connect this to what we already know. Taking \(f=1\) recovers the arc length \(\int_C ds=\int_a^b \lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\,dt\) of Section 1.1—total length is the “mass” of a wire of density one. And \(\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\) is the speed \(\left|\mathbf{v}(t)\right|\) from that section, so the factor converts time spent into distance covered.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3. Mass and length of a helical wire.

    Consider a wire of length \(l\) and mass \(m\text{.}\) The wire is helix-shaped, with its bottom end located at \((1,0,0)\) and parametric equation
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t)=\langle \cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle,\qquad t\ge 0. \end{equation*}
    Its density (mass per unit length) is \(\rho(x,y,z)=4x^2y^2\) kg/m. Assuming that \(m=\dfrac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}\) kg, calculate the length \(l\) of the wire.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The wire occupies the part of the helix with \(0\le t\le b\text{,}\) where the upper limit \(b\) is unknown: it marks where the wire ends. Our plan has two steps. First we use the known mass in the formula
    \begin{equation} m = \int_0^{b} \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\right\rVert\,dt\tag{1.1} \end{equation}
    to find \(b\text{;}\) then the length is the arc length \(l=\int_0^b \lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\,dt\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First we find \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) and its length:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{r}'(t) \amp = \langle -\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 1\rangle,\\ \left\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\right\rVert \amp = \sqrt{\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t+1}=\sqrt{2}. \end{align*}
    Next we write the density along the curve in terms of \(t\text{,}\) using the double-angle identity \(2\sin t\cos t=\sin(2t)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) = 4x^2y^2 = 4\cos^2 t\,\sin^2 t = \left(2\sin t\cos t\right)^2 = \sin^2(2t). \end{equation*}
    Substituting into (1.1) and using the half-angle identity \(\sin^2(2t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1-\cos(4t)\bigr)\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} m \amp = \sqrt{2}\int_0^{b}\sin^2(2t)\,dt = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_0^{b}\bigl(1-\cos(4t)\bigr)\,dt\\ \amp = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(t-\frac{1}{4}\sin(4t)\right)\bigg|_0^{b} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(b-\frac{1}{4}\sin(4b)\right). \end{align*}
    Setting this equal to the given mass \(m=\dfrac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}\) and multiplying both sides by \(\sqrt{2}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} b-\frac{1}{4}\sin(4b)=\frac{\pi}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b=\frac{\pi}{2}, \end{equation*}
    since \(\sin(4\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2})=\sin(2\pi)=0\text{.}\) (This solution is the only one: the left-hand side has derivative \(1-\cos(4b)\ge 0\text{,}\) so it is increasing and crosses the value \(\pi/2\) exactly once.)
    🔗
    The length of the wire is then the arc length from \(t=0\) to \(t=\pi/2\text{.}\) Because the speed is the constant \(\sqrt{2}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} l=\int_0^{\pi/2}\left\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\right\rVert\,dt =\sqrt{2}\int_0^{\pi/2}dt =\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\approx 2.22 \text{ meters}. \end{equation*}
    The wire—a quarter turn of the helix from \((1,0,0)\) up to \((0,1,\pi/2)\)—is shown in Figure 1.4, and the accumulation of its mass is animated in Figure 1.5.
