    Section 3.3 The Divergence Theorem

    In Section 3.1 we saw that the divergence of a two-dimensional field measures its flux density, and Green’s theorem (in its normal form) converted a flux integral around a closed curve into a double integral of the divergence. In this section we extend both ideas to three dimensions: the result is Gauss’s divergence theorem, which converts a flux integral over a closed surface—often a lengthy parametrized computation, as in Section 2.10—into an ordinary triple integral.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.3.1 Divergence in 3D

    Recall from (3.1.2) that in 2D the divergence of \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\) is \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \partial M/\partial x + \partial N/\partial y\text{,}\) the flux density of the field at a point: positive where the gas expands, negative where it is compressed (see Example 3.1.4). The three-dimensional definition adds one term.
    🔗

    Definition 3.3.1.

    Consider the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = M(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{j} + P(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{k}\text{.}\) Then the divergence of \(\mathbf{F}\) is defined as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}.\tag{3.3.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    If the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) represents the velocity field of a flowing gas, then the divergence of \(\mathbf{F}\) corresponds to the amount of compression or expansion of the gas at the point \((x,y,z)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 3.3.2. Expanding or Compressing?

    Consider the following velocity fields of a gas, and in each case determine whether the gas is expanding or compressing.
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle \mathbf{F} = x\,\mathbf{i} + y\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \mathbf{F} = -x\,\mathbf{i} - y\,\mathbf{j} - z\,\mathbf{k}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \mathbf{F} = -y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    4. \(\displaystyle \mathbf{F} = \big( x\,\mathbf{i} + y\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k} \big) / \big( x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \big)^{3/2}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We calculate the divergence of the given vector fields in each case using (3.3.1).
    🔗
    Part A. \(\mathbf{F} = x\,\mathbf{i} + y\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k} \Rightarrow \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 \gt 0\text{:}\) the gas is expanding, at the same rate at every point. This radial field is shown on the left in Figure 3.3.4.
    🔗
    Part B. \(\mathbf{F} = -x\,\mathbf{i} - y\,\mathbf{j} - z\,\mathbf{k} \Rightarrow \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = -3 \lt 0\text{:}\) the gas is being compressed; the field is the reverse of the one in part A, with all arrows pointing toward the origin.
    🔗
    Part C. \(\mathbf{F} = -y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j} \Rightarrow \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0\text{:}\) the gas is neither expanding nor being compressed—this field rotates rigidly about the \(z\)-axis, as on the right in Figure 3.3.4.
    🔗
    Part D. Writing \(\rho^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2\text{,}\) a direct computation with the quotient rule gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{3\,(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2} - 3\,(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}} {(x^2+y^2+z^2)^3} = 0. \end{equation*}
    Again, the gas is neither expanding nor being compressed: even though the arrows point outward, they weaken with distance at exactly the rate that preserves volume. (This is the field of a point charge in electrostatics.) The animation in Figure 3.3.3 shows all four fields.

    Subsection 3.3.2 Gauss’s Divergence Theorem

    The divergence theorem relates the outward flux through the surface boundary of a 3D region to the triple integral over the volume of the enclosed region.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.3.5. Gauss’s Divergence Theorem.

    Let \(\mathbf{F}\) be a vector field whose components have continuous first partial derivatives, and let \(S\) be a piecewise smooth oriented closed surface. The flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across \(S\) in the direction of the surface’s outward unit normal field \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) equals the integral of \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\) over the region \(D\) enclosed by the surface:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \underbrace{\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma}_{\text{outward flux through the boundary } S} = \underbrace{\iiint_D \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\, dV}_{\text{triple integral of the divergence over } D}.\tag{3.3.2} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Proof.

    Write \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j} + P\,\mathbf{k}\text{.}\) Both sides of (3.3.2) break into three pieces along the three components: by (3.3.1) the right-hand side is the sum of the integrals of \(\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial x}\text{,}\) \(\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial y}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial z}\text{,}\) and the left-hand side is the sum of the fluxes of \(M\,\mathbf{i}\text{,}\) \(N\,\mathbf{j}\) and \(P\,\mathbf{k}\text{.}\) So it is enough to prove the three separate statements
    🔗
    \begin{align} \iint_S M\,\mathbf{i} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iiint_D \frac{\partial M}{\partial x}\, dV ,\tag{3.3.3}\\ \iint_S N\,\mathbf{j} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iiint_D \frac{\partial N}{\partial y}\, dV ,\notag\\ \iint_S P\,\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iiint_D \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\, dV ,\notag \end{align}
    and add them. We prove the third; the other two are the same argument with the axes renamed.
    🔗
    Step 1: a solid that is simple in the \(z\)-direction. Suppose first that \(D\) can be described as in Subsection 2.5.2, that is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} D = \left\{(x,y,z) : (x,y) \in R,\ u_1(x,y) \le z \le u_2(x,y)\right\} , \end{equation*}
    where \(R\) is the shadow of \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane and \(u_1 \le u_2\) are smooth. Its boundary \(S\) then falls into three pieces, drawn in Figure 3.3.6: the cap \(S_2\) where \(z = u_2(x,y)\text{,}\) the floor \(S_1\) where \(z = u_1(x,y)\text{,}\) and the vertical wall \(S_3\) standing over the boundary of \(R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On the wall the outward normal is horizontal, so \(\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = 0\) and \(S_3\) contributes nothing. On the cap the outward normal points upward, and for a graph \(z = u_2(x,y)\) we already know from (2.10.6) that \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \left\langle -\dfrac{\partial u_2}{\partial x},\ -\dfrac{\partial u_2}{\partial y},\ 1 \right\rangle dx\, dy\text{,}\) so that \(\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = dx\, dy\text{.}\) On the floor the outward normal points downward, which flips the sign, and \(\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = -dx\, dy\text{.}\) Hence
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \iint_S P\,\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iint_{S_2} P\,\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma + \iint_{S_1} P\,\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma\\ \amp = \iint_R P\bigl(x,y,u_2(x,y)\bigr) dx\, dy\\ \amp \qquad - \iint_R P\bigl(x,y,u_1(x,y)\bigr) dx\, dy . \end{align*}
    Step 2: the triple integral. Integrating first in \(z\text{,}\) as in (2.5.3), and using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus on the inner integral,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \iiint_D \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\, dV \amp = \iint_R \left[\int_{u_1(x,y)}^{u_2(x,y)} \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\, dz\right] dx\, dy\\ \amp = \iint_R \Bigl[P\bigl(x,y,u_2(x,y)\bigr) - P\bigl(x,y,u_1(x,y)\bigr)\Bigr] dx\, dy , \end{align*}
    which is exactly what Step 1 produced. So the third statement of (3.3.3) holds for such a solid. Running the same argument with \(D\) viewed as simple in the \(x\)-direction gives the first statement, and in the \(y\)-direction the second. Adding the three proves the theorem for any solid that is simple in all three directions.
    🔗
    Step 3: general solids. A solid that is not simple in all three directions is cut into finitely many pieces that are, exactly as a region of the plane was cut in Subsection 2.2.1. Apply the theorem to each piece and add. Every cut surface is shared by two neighbouring pieces, and it carries opposite outward normals as seen from the two sides, so the two flux contributions differ only in sign and cancel. What survives on the left is the flux through the original boundary \(S\text{,}\) and the volume integrals simply add up to the integral over \(D\text{.}\) This is the same bookkeeping that made the loop property of Section 1.5 work, one dimension up.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.8. Flux Through a Sphere, the Easy Way.

    Use the divergence theorem to compute the flux of the vector field \(\mathbf{F} = x\,\mathbf{i} + y\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k}\) through the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    We calculate the right-hand side of the divergence theorem, (3.3.2). Since \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 3\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_D \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\, dV = 3 \iiint_D dV = 3\, (\text{volume of the sphere}) = 3 \left( \frac{4}{3}\pi \right) = 4\pi. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \iiint_D \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\, dV = 4\pi, \end{equation*}
    with no parametrization, no cross product, and no double integral in sight: the field and its outward-pointing flux are shown in Figure 3.3.9.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.10. Revisiting the Flux of \(\langle z, y, x \rangle\).

    In Example 2.10.11 we found the flux of \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = z\,\mathbf{i} + y\,\mathbf{j} + x\,\mathbf{k}\) across the unit sphere by parametrizing the surface, computing \(\mathbf{r}_\phi \times \mathbf{r}_\theta\text{,}\) and integrating—a full page of work that produced \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\text{.}\) Recompute this flux using the divergence theorem.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    The divergence is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial x}{\partial z} = 0 + 1 + 0 = 1, \end{equation*}
    so by (3.3.2), with \(S\) the unit sphere and \(D\) the unit ball \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 1\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \iiint_D \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\, dV = \iiint_D dV = \text{volume of the sphere} = \frac{4\pi}{3}(1)^3 = \frac{4\pi}{3}, \end{equation*}
    in agreement with the long computation—in one line. Watch the two sphere computations side by side in Figure 3.3.11.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3.12. Revisiting the Paraboloid, the Easy Way.

    Evaluate the surface integral \(\displaystyle \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k}\) and the surface \(S\) is the boundary of the solid region \(E\) enclosed by the paraboloid \(z = 1 - x^2 - y^2\) and the plane \(z = 0\text{,}\) this time using the divergence theorem.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 3.
    In Example 2.10.13 we computed this flux by splitting \(S\) into the cap \(S_1\) and the disk \(S_2\text{,}\) finding a normal vector for each piece, and evaluating two surface integrals. With the divergence theorem the whole computation collapses. The divergence is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial y}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial x}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial z} = 0 + 0 + 1 = 1, \end{equation*}
    so by (3.3.2), using cylindrical coordinates for the solid \(E\) (\(0 \le z \le 1 - r^2\text{,}\) \(0 \le r \le 1\)),
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp= \iiint_E \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\, dV = \iiint_E dV = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\! \int_0^1\!\! \int_0^{1-r^2} r\, dz\, dr\, d\theta\\ \amp= 2\pi \int_0^1 (1 - r^2)\, r\, dr = 2\pi \left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} \right) = \frac{\pi}{2}, \end{align*}
    which agrees with the two-piece surface computation. Whenever the surface is closed and the divergence is simple, the divergence theorem is the tool of choice; see Figure 3.3.13.
