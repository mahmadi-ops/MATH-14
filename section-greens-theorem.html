    Section 3.1 Green’s Theorem in the Plane

    Green’s theorem comes in two forms: one about how much of a vector field flows across a closed curve, and one about how much flows along it. Each needs an operation on vector fields of its own, divergence for the first and curl for the second. So the section has two halves, built in the same order. Each starts with its line integral, computed with a unit vector attached to the curve: the outward normal \(\mathbf{n}\) for the first, the unit tangent \(\mathbf{T}\) for the second. Each then derives its operation from that integral around a small rectangle, and ends with the form of Green’s theorem that connects the two when the curve is closed, followed by its examples. The Concept Check at the end uses both.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.1.1 Flux and Divergence

    Subsubsection 3.1.1.1 The Flux Across a Plane Curve

    The first form of Green’s theorem, in Subsubsection 3.1.1.3, measures how much of a field flows across a closed curve \(C\text{:}\) its outward flux \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds\text{,}\) the line integral of the component of \(\mathbf{F}\) along the outward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) of the curve. So it uses \(\mathbf{n}\) rather than the unit tangent \(\mathbf{T}\) of (1.4.7), introduced in Section 1.4. Suppose \(C\) is traversed counterclockwise and parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = x(t)\,\mathbf{i} + y(t)\,\mathbf{j}\text{.}\) Its velocity, the tangent vector of (1.1.14), is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt} \right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(\mathbf{n}\) is a unit normal to the curve, it is perpendicular to the tangent vector \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The vector \(\left\langle \frac{dy}{dt},\ -\frac{dx}{dt} \right\rangle\) has this property, because
    \begin{align*} \amp \left\langle \frac{dy}{dt},\ -\frac{dx}{dt} \right\rangle \cdot \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt} \right\rangle\\ \amp \quad = \frac{dy}{dt}\,\frac{dx}{dt} - \frac{dx}{dt}\,\frac{dy}{dt} = 0. \end{align*}
    It is \(\mathbf{v}\) turned a quarter turn clockwise. Of the two directions perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) this is the one that points out of the region: as \(C\) is traversed counterclockwise, the region \(R\) it encloses stays on the left, so turning \(\mathbf{v}\) clockwise makes it point to the right, out of \(R\text{.}\) (The opposite vector, \(\left\langle -\frac{dy}{dt},\ \frac{dx}{dt} \right\rangle\text{,}\) points into \(R\text{.}\)) Therefore the outward normal is
    \begin{equation*} \left\langle \frac{dy}{dt},\ -\frac{dx}{dt} \right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To find the unit normal we divide this vector by its length. Its length is \(\sqrt{\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2} = \left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert\text{,}\) the same as the length of \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation} \mathbf{n} = \frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} \left\langle \frac{dy}{dt},\ -\frac{dx}{dt} \right\rangle.\tag{3.1.1} \end{equation}
    Compare it with the unit tangent \(\mathbf{T} = \frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt} \right\rangle\) of (1.4.7): the two have the same components, swapped, with one sign changed.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.1. The Unit Normal to the Unit Circle.

    Find the outward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) and the unit tangent \(\mathbf{T}\) of the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Parametrize the circle as in (1.1.3):
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t) = (\cos t)\,\mathbf{i} + (\sin t)\,\mathbf{j}, \quad 0 \le t \le 2\pi, \end{equation*}
    which traverses it counterclockwise. Then \(\frac{dx}{dt} = -\sin t\) and \(\frac{dy}{dt} = \cos t\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{v} \amp = \left\langle -\sin t,\ \cos t \right\rangle,\\ \left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert \amp = \sqrt{\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t} = 1. \end{align*}
    🔗
    By (3.1.1) and (1.4.7),
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{n} \amp= \frac{1}{1} \left\langle \cos t,\ -(-\sin t) \right\rangle\\ \amp= \left\langle \cos t,\ \sin t \right\rangle,\\ \mathbf{T} \amp= \frac{1}{1} \left\langle -\sin t,\ \cos t \right\rangle\\ \amp= \left\langle -\sin t,\ \cos t \right\rangle. \end{align*}
    🔗
    On the unit circle the outward unit normal is the position vector \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) itself: it points straight away from the center, as it should. As a check, \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{T} = -\cos t\sin t + \sin t\cos t = 0\) and \(\left\lVert\mathbf{n}\right\rVert = 1\text{.}\) For instance, at \(t = \pi/6\) we get \(\mathbf{n} = \left\langle \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\ \frac{1}{2} \right\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{T} = \left\langle -\frac{1}{2},\ \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \right\rangle\text{.}\) Both vectors are drawn at three points of the circle in Figure 3.1.2.
    🔗
    🔗
    This is where the line integral \(\oint_C M\,dy - N\,dx\) in the normal form of Green’s theorem, (3.1.4), comes from. For \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\text{,}\) the line element \(ds = \left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert\,dt\) of (1.2.3) cancels the length in (3.1.1):
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds\\ \amp \quad = \frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} \left( M\,\frac{dy}{dt} - N\,\frac{dx}{dt} \right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert\,dt\\ \amp \quad = M\,dy - N\,dx. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.1.1.2 Divergence

    Consider a vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) to be the velocity field of a fluid. How can we find the net flow rate of the gas through the boundaries of a rectangle shown in Figure 3.1.3? It is the outward flux \(\oint \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds\) of Subsubsection 3.1.1.1 around the rectangle, and on a rectangle the outward unit normal can be read off each side: \(\mathbf{j}\) on the top, \(-\mathbf{j}\) on the bottom, \(\mathbf{i}\) on the right and \(-\mathbf{i}\) on the left.
    🔗
    Let us evaluate the flux through each side, assuming the field is nearly constant along each side:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \text{Top:} \amp\quad \mathbf{F}(x, y+\Delta y) \cdot \mathbf{j}\, \Delta x\\ \amp\qquad = N(x, y+\Delta y)\, \Delta x\\ \text{Bottom:} \amp\quad \mathbf{F}(x, y) \cdot (-\mathbf{j})\, \Delta x\\ \amp\qquad = -N(x, y)\, \Delta x\\ \text{Right:} \amp\quad \mathbf{F}(x+\Delta x, y) \cdot \mathbf{i}\, \Delta y\\ \amp\qquad = M(x+\Delta x, y)\, \Delta y\\ \text{Left:} \amp\quad \mathbf{F}(x, y) \cdot (-\mathbf{i})\, \Delta y\\ \amp\qquad = -M(x, y)\, \Delta y. \end{align*}
    The net flow rate is approximately
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} \right) \Delta x\, \Delta y. \end{equation*}
    Note that this is an approximation, since we are assuming the velocity field has a constant value along each side of the rectangle. The flux density can be calculated as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{align} \amp \text{Flux density}\notag\\ \amp = \frac{\text{net flux through the rectangle}}{\text{area of the rectangle}}\notag\\ \amp = \frac{\left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} \right) \Delta x \Delta y}{\Delta x \Delta y}\notag\\ \amp = \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y}.\tag{3.1.2} \end{align}
    Note that, in the limit that \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\) approach \(0\text{,}\) (3.1.2) represents to what extent a gas is expanding or compressing at each point. This quantity is known as the divergence, and it is denoted by \(\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F}\text{.}\) Watch the construction in Figure 3.1.5.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.4.

    The divergence of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) is defined as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y},\tag{3.1.3} \end{equation}
    where \(\nabla = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\,\mathbf{i} + \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\,\mathbf{j}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.6. Computing and Interpreting Divergence.

    The vector fields below represent the velocity field of a gas. Calculate the divergence in each case and explain what it means physically.
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle \mathbf{F}(x,y) = 2x\,\mathbf{i} + 2y\,\mathbf{j}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \mathbf{F}(x,y) = \dfrac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,\mathbf{i} + \dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,\mathbf{j}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Part A. Using (3.1.3),
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\\ \amp \quad = \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\,\mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\,\mathbf{j} \right) \cdot \left( 2x\,\mathbf{i} + 2y\,\mathbf{j} \right)\\ \amp \quad = 2 + 2 = 4 \gt 0. \end{align*}
    Note that for every point on the \(xy\)-plane we have found that \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} \gt 0\text{,}\) which means that the gas is expanding, since there is a positive flux density at every point \((x,y)\text{.}\) The field is shown on the left in Figure 3.1.7.
    🔗
    Part B. Again by (3.1.3),
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\\ \amp \quad = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \frac{x}{x^2+y^2} \right)\\ \amp \quad = \frac{2xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2} - \frac{2xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = 0. \end{align*}
    Note that for every point on the \(xy\)-plane we have found that \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0\text{,}\) which means that the gas is neither expanding nor compressing. This rotating field is shown on the right in Figure 3.1.7.

    Subsubsection 3.1.1.3 Green’s Theorem: The Flux-Divergence (Normal) Form

    We can now state Green’s theorem in 2D
     1 
    Note that we only cover Green’s theorem in 2D; however, you should know that this theorem can be generalized to 3D, and in that case it is known as Stokes’ theorem, which is Section 3.2.
    . It comes in two forms, and the first, the flux form, relates the outward flux across a simple
     2 
    A curve in the \(xy\)-plane is simple if it does not cross itself.
    closed curve \(C\) and the double integral of the divergence over the region \(R\) bounded by the curve \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.8. Green’s Theorem (Flux-Divergence or Normal Form).

    Let \(C\) be a piecewise smooth, simple closed curve enclosing a region \(R\) in the plane. Let \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\) be a vector field with \(M\) and \(N\) having continuous first partial derivatives in an open region containing \(R\text{.}\) Then the outward flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across \(C\) equals the double integral of \(\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F}\) over the region \(R\) enclosed by \(C\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align} \amp \underbrace{\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}\, ds}_{\text{outward flux}} = \oint_C M\, dy - N\, dx\notag\\ \amp \quad = \underbrace{\iint_R \left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy}_{\substack{\text{integral of} \\ \operatorname{div}\mathbf{F} \text{ over } R}}.\tag{3.1.4} \end{align}
    🔗

    Example 3.1.10. Verifying the Normal Form on the Unit Circle.

    Assume that the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (x - y)\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\) and the closed curve \(C\) is the unit circle centered at the origin, drawn in Figure 3.1.11. Convince yourself of the normal form of Green’s theorem, (3.1.4), by applying it to this example.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We begin by noticing that the unit circle can be parametrized as
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp C: \quad \mathbf{r}(t) = (\cos t)\,\mathbf{i} + (\sin t)\,\mathbf{j},\\ \amp \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi. \end{align*}
    After substituting the parametrized equations \(x(t) = \cos t\) and \(y(t) = \sin t\) into the vector field, we get \(\mathbf{F} = (\cos t - \sin t)\,\mathbf{i} + (\cos t)\,\mathbf{j}\text{,}\) and thus
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M \amp = \cos t - \sin t,\\ N \amp = \cos t,\\ dx \amp = d(\cos t) = -\sin t\, dt,\\ dy \amp = d(\sin t) = \cos t\, dt, \end{align*}
    with the partial derivatives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} = 0. \end{equation*}
    For the line integral in (3.1.4) we have
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C M\, dy - N\, dx\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^{2\pi} (\cos t - \sin t)(\cos t\, dt)\\ \amp \qquad {} - (\cos t)(-\sin t\, dt)\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2 t\, dt = \pi, \end{align*}
    which is the same integral we get from the outward unit normal of Example 3.1.1: there \(\mathbf{n} = (\cos t)\,\mathbf{i} + (\sin t)\,\mathbf{j}\) and \(ds = dt\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds = \big((\cos t - \sin t)\cos t + \cos t \sin t\big)\,dt = \cos^2 t\, dt\text{.}\) The double integral gives the same value:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_R \left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy\\ \amp \quad = \iint_R (1 + 0)\, dx\, dy\\ \amp \quad = \text{area inside the unit circle} = \pi. \end{align*}
    So the outward flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across the unit circle is \(\pi\text{,}\) computed either way.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.1.2 Circulation and Curl

    Subsubsection 3.1.2.1 The Circulation Around a Plane Curve

    The second form of Green’s theorem, in Subsubsection 3.1.2.3, measures how much of a field flows along a closed curve \(C\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise. Where the first form used the outward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\text{,}\) this one uses the unit tangent \(\mathbf{T}\text{,}\) and both the vector and the integral are ones we have already computed.
    🔗
    The unit tangent is the velocity divided by its length, (1.4.7) in Subsection 1.4.3: for \(\mathbf{r}(t) = x(t)\,\mathbf{i} + y(t)\,\mathbf{j}\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{T} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} = \frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt} \right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    It points in the direction of travel, a quarter turn counterclockwise from the outward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) of (3.1.1). Example 3.1.1 finds both vectors for the unit circle, and Figure 3.1.2 draws them.
    🔗
    The integral is the line integral of a vector field from Subsection 1.4.3. There the work done by a force field along a curve came out as \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds\text{,}\) (1.4.6): the component of \(\mathbf{F}\) along \(\mathbf{T}\text{,}\) integrated with respect to arc length. For any vector field the same integral is called its circulation along the curve, and around a closed curve it is the circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around \(C\text{,}\) written \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To compute it, the line element \(ds = \left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert\,dt\) of (1.2.3) cancels the length in \(\mathbf{T}\text{,}\) just as it did for \(\mathbf{n}\text{.}\) For \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds\\ \amp \quad = \frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} \left( M\,\frac{dx}{dt} + N\,\frac{dy}{dt} \right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert\,dt\\ \amp \quad = M\,dx + N\,dy, \end{align*}
    so that
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds \amp = \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\\ \amp = \oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy, \end{align*}
    the differential form of (1.4.10). This is where the line integral \(\oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy\) in the tangential form of Green’s theorem, (3.1.6), comes from.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.1.2.2 Curl

    Again, let us consider a vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) to be the velocity field of a fluid. This time we are interested in answering this question: how can we figure out the circulation density of the fluid at any point on the \(xy\)-plane?
    🔗
    To answer this question, we compute the flow rates of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) along the sides of the rectangle (counterclockwise), as in Figure 3.1.12: the circulation \(\oint \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds\) of Subsubsection 3.1.2.1 around the rectangle, where the unit tangent can be read off each side: \(\mathbf{i}\) along the bottom, \(\mathbf{j}\) up the right side, \(-\mathbf{i}\) along the top and \(-\mathbf{j}\) down the left side. Then we take the limit as \(\Delta x, \Delta y \to 0\) to find the circulation density at a point in the \(xy\)-plane.
    🔗
    We begin by computing the flow rates along each edge, i.e. top, bottom, left, and right:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \text{Top:} \amp\quad \mathbf{F}(x, y+\Delta y) \cdot (-\mathbf{i})\, \Delta x\\ \amp\qquad = -M(x, y+\Delta y)\, \Delta x\\ \text{Bottom:} \amp\quad \mathbf{F}(x, y) \cdot \mathbf{i}\, \Delta x\\ \amp\qquad = M(x, y)\, \Delta x\\ \text{Right:} \amp\quad \mathbf{F}(x+\Delta x, y) \cdot \mathbf{j}\, \Delta y\\ \amp\qquad = N(x+\Delta x, y)\, \Delta y\\ \text{Left:} \amp\quad \mathbf{F}(x, y) \cdot (-\mathbf{j})\, \Delta y\\ \amp\qquad = -N(x, y)\, \Delta y. \end{align*}
    Then we sum up the flow across the parallel edges:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \text{Top and bottom:}\\ \amp \quad -\big( M(x, y+\Delta y) - M(x,y) \big) \Delta x\\ \amp \qquad \approx -\left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\, \Delta y \right) \Delta x\\ \amp \text{Right and left:}\\ \amp \quad \big( N(x+\Delta x, y) - N(x,y) \big) \Delta y\\ \amp \qquad \approx \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\, \Delta x \right) \Delta y. \end{align*}
    Finally, after summing the flow rates above and taking the limit as \(\Delta x, \Delta y \to 0\text{,}\) for the circulation density we get
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \frac{\text{circulation around rectangle}}{\text{rectangle area}}\\ \amp \quad \approx \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}. \end{align*}
    This quantity is denoted by \(\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{k}\) and is known as the \(k\)-component of curl.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.13.

    The \(k\)-component of the curl of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{k} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}.\tag{3.1.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    To find out the circulation density at a point in the \(xy\)-plane, you can imagine a paddle wheel whose axis goes through the point of interest. Then the \(k\)-component of curl is positive if the fluid flow causes the paddle wheel to rotate counterclockwise, and it is negative if it turns clockwise. The animation in Figure 3.1.14 shows both behaviors.

    Subsubsection 3.1.2.3 Green’s Theorem: The Circulation-Curl (Tangential) Form

    The second form of Green’s theorem connects the counterclockwise circulation of a vector field around a simple closed curve \(C\) and the double integral of the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of the curl of the field over the region \(R\) enclosed by the curve.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.17. Green’s Theorem (Circulation-Curl or Tangential Form).

    Let \(C\) be a piecewise smooth, simple closed curve enclosing a region \(R\) in the plane. Let \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\) be a vector field with \(M\) and \(N\) having continuous first partial derivatives in an open region containing \(R\text{.}\) Then the counterclockwise circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around \(C\) equals the double integral of \((\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{k}\) over \(R\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align} \amp \underbrace{\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{T}\, ds}_{\text{circulation}} = \oint_C M\, dx + N\, dy\notag\\ \amp \quad = \underbrace{\iint_R \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy}_{\substack{\text{integral of} \\ \operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{k} \text{ over } R}}.\tag{3.1.6} \end{align}
    🔗

    Proof.
    Write \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\text{.}\) Both sides of (3.1.6) split into two pieces, one carrying \(M\) and one carrying \(N\text{,}\) so it is enough to prove the two separate statements
    🔗
    \begin{align} \oint_C M\,dx \amp = -\iint_R \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\,dA ,\tag{3.1.7}\\ \oint_C N\,dy \amp = \iint_R \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\,dA ,\notag \end{align}
    and add them. Note the minus sign in the first: it is the whole reason the curl carries one.
    🔗
    Step 1: the \(M\) statement, on a region cut by vertical lines. Suppose first that \(R\) is a region of the kind studied in Subsection 2.2.1,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} R = \bigl\{ (x,y) : \amp\ a \le x \le b,\\ \amp\ f_2(x) \le y \le f_1(x) \bigr\} , \end{align*}
    so that \(C\) is made of the bottom curve \(y = f_2(x)\) travelled left to right, the top curve \(y = f_1(x)\) travelled right to left, and possibly two vertical segments at \(x=a\) and \(x=b\text{.}\) This is the picture in Figure 3.1.18. On the vertical segments \(x\) never changes, so \(dx = 0\) and they contribute nothing to \(\oint_C M\,dx\text{.}\) What is left is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C M\,dx\\ \amp = \int_a^b M\bigl(x,f_2(x)\bigr)\,dx\\ \amp \quad {} + \int_b^a M\bigl(x,f_1(x)\bigr)\,dx\\ \amp = -\int_a^b \Bigl[M\bigl(x,f_1(x)\bigr)\\ \amp \qquad {} - M\bigl(x,f_2(x)\bigr)\Bigr] dx , \end{align*}
    the sign appearing because the top is travelled backwards. Meanwhile, doing the double integral in the \(y\)-direction first and applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to the inner integral,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_R \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\,dA\\ \amp = \int_a^b\left[\int_{f_2(x)}^{f_1(x)} \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\,dy\right] dx\\ \amp = \int_a^b \Bigl[M\bigl(x,f_1(x)\bigr) - M\bigl(x,f_2(x)\bigr)\Bigr] dx . \end{align*}
    The two agree up to the minus sign, which is the first line of (3.1.7).
    🔗
    Step 2: the \(N\) statement. Now read \(R\) the other way, as in Subsection 2.2.2, with \(c \le y \le d\) and \(g_1(y) \le x \le g_2(y)\text{.}\) On the horizontal parts of the boundary \(dy = 0\text{,}\) and the same computation with the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) exchanged gives the second line of (3.1.7). Here the right-hand curve is travelled upwards, in the direction of increasing \(y\text{,}\) which is why no minus sign appears. Adding the two lines proves the theorem for a region that can be described both ways.
    🔗
    Step 3: general regions. A region that cannot be described both ways at once is cut into finitely many pieces that can, exactly as in Subsection 2.2.1, and the theorem is applied to each piece. Each cut is travelled twice, once for the piece on either side, and in opposite directions, so those two line integrals cancel. What survives is the integral around the original curve \(C\text{,}\) while the double integrals add up to the integral over \(R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.1.20. Verifying the Tangential Form on the Unit Circle.

    For the same vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (x - y)\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\) and the same unit circle \(C\) as in Example 3.1.10, convince yourself of the tangential form of Green’s theorem, (3.1.6), by applying it to this example.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    With the parametrization \(\mathbf{r}(t) = (\cos t)\,\mathbf{i} + (\sin t)\,\mathbf{j}\text{,}\) \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\text{,}\) of Example 3.1.10 we again have
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M = \cos t - \sin t, \amp\qquad dx = -\sin t\, dt,\\ N = \cos t, \amp\qquad dy = \cos t\, dt, \end{align*}
    and this time we need the other two partial derivatives,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = -1, \qquad \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} = 1. \end{equation*}
    The tangential form of Green’s theorem, (3.1.6), can then be verified as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C M\, dx + N\, dy\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^{2\pi} (\cos t - \sin t)(-\sin t\, dt)\\ \amp \qquad {} + (\cos t)(\cos t\, dt)\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^{2\pi} (-\sin t \cos t + 1)\, dt = 2\pi, \end{align*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_R \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy\\ \amp \quad = \iint_R \big( 1 - (-1) \big)\, dx\, dy\\ \amp \quad = 2 \iint_R dx\, dy = 2\pi. \end{align*}
    The field and the curve are shown in Figure 3.1.21; note that the field has a positive (counterclockwise) circulation, which we calculated above to be \(2\pi\text{.}\) Watch both computations, the flux of Example 3.1.10 and this circulation, side by side in Figure 3.1.28.

    Example 3.1.22. A Line Integral via Green’s Theorem and Substitution.

    Evaluate the line integral
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \frac{dx}{x+y} - \frac{dy}{x+y}, \end{equation*}
    where \(C\) is the boundary of the square with vertices \((1,0)\text{,}\) \((2,1)\text{,}\) \((1,2)\text{,}\) and \((0,1)\text{,}\) shown in Figure 3.1.24. Make suitable substitutions in your integral to make the limits of integration numbers as opposed to functions.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    In this example we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} M(x,y) = \frac{1}{x+y}, \quad N(x,y) = \frac{-1}{x+y}, \end{equation*}
    and thus
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\\ \amp \quad = \frac{1}{(x+y)^2} - \frac{-1}{(x+y)^2}\\ \amp \quad = \frac{2}{(x+y)^2}. \end{align*}
    Then we can use the tangential form of Green’s theorem, (3.1.6), to evaluate the line integral:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C M\, dx + N\, dy\\ \amp \quad = \iint_R \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy\\ \amp \quad = \iint_R \frac{2}{(x+y)^2}\, dx\, dy. \end{align*}
    The region \(R\) is the square bounded by the four lines \(y = x + 1\text{,}\) \(y = x - 1\text{,}\) \(y = -x + 1\text{,}\) and \(y = -x + 3\text{,}\) so the natural substitution uses the boundary expressions:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u = x + y, \qquad v = y - x, \end{equation*}
    under which the region becomes the rectangle \(1 \le u \le 3\text{,}\) \(-1 \le v \le 1\text{.}\) Solving for \(x\) and \(y\) gives \(x = \tfrac{u-v}{2}\text{,}\) \(y = \tfrac{u+v}{2}\text{,}\) so the Jacobian is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \begin{vmatrix} \tfrac{1}{2} \amp -\tfrac{1}{2} \\ \tfrac{1}{2} \amp \tfrac{1}{2} \end{vmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_R \frac{2}{(x+y)^2}\, dx\, dy\\ \amp \quad = \int_{-1}^{1} \int_1^3 \frac{2}{u^2} \cdot \frac{1}{2}\, du\, dv\\ \amp \quad = \int_{-1}^{1} \left[ -\frac{1}{u} \right]_1^3 dv\\ \amp \quad = \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{2}{3}\, dv = \frac{4}{3}. \end{align*}
    Green’s theorem converted the line integral into a double integral, and the substitution—exactly the technique of the previous section—made its limits constant. See Figure 3.1.23 for the animation.

    Subsubsection 3.1.2.4 An Alternative Form of the Component Test for Conservative Fields

    The component test tells whether a field is conservative from its partial derivatives alone:
    🔗

    Component Test for Conservative Fields.

    Let \(\mathbf{F} = M(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{j} + P(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{k}\) be a field on a connected and simply connected domain whose component functions have continuous first partial derivatives. Then \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative if and only if
    \begin{align} \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \amp = \frac{\partial N}{\partial z},\notag\\ \frac{\partial M}{\partial z} \amp = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x}, \quad \text{and}\notag\\ \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} \amp = \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}.\tag{3.1.8} \end{align}
    🔗
    🔗
    The three equalities of (3.1.8) are equivalent to \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\text{,}\) since
    🔗
    \begin{align} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} \amp \mathbf{j} \amp \mathbf{k} \\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ M \amp N \amp P \end{vmatrix}\notag\\ \amp = \left( \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial N}{\partial z} \right) \mathbf{i}\notag\\ \amp \quad {} + \left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} \right) \mathbf{j}\notag\\ \amp \quad {} + \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) \mathbf{k}.\tag{3.1.9} \end{align}
    So to check whether a vector field is conservative or not, we can check whether its curl is zero or not.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.25. Conservative Fields and Green’s Theorem.

    Consider the line integral \(\displaystyle \oint_C 4x^3 y\, dx + x^4\, dy\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. As you know, the vector form of a line integral is \(\displaystyle \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\text{.}\) Write down the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) for the above line integral.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Is this vector field a conservative field? Use the component test, (3.1.8), to answer.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Using Green’s theorem, confirm your answer to part B.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Part A. The vector field is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{F}(x,y) \amp = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\\ \amp = 4x^3 y\,\mathbf{i} + x^4\,\mathbf{j}. \end{align*}
    Part B. Treating \(\mathbf{F}\) as a field in space with \(P = 0\) in (3.1.9),
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} \amp \mathbf{j} \amp \mathbf{k} \\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ M \amp N \amp 0 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) \mathbf{k}\\ \amp = \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^4) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(4x^3 y) \right) \mathbf{k}\\ \amp = \left( 4x^3 - 4x^3 \right)\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{0}, \end{align*}
    and hence the vector field is conservative.
    🔗
    Part C. Using the tangential form of Green’s theorem, (3.1.6), for any simple closed curve \(C\) enclosing a region \(D\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C 4x^3 y\, dx + x^4\, dy\\ \amp \quad = \iint_D \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy\\ \amp \quad = \iint_D \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^4) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(4x^3 y) \right) dx\, dy\\ \amp \quad = 0. \end{align*}
    The circulation of a conservative field around every closed curve vanishes, which confirms part B. The animation in Figure 3.1.26 shows the field and several closed loops, all with zero circulation.

    Checkpoint 3.1.29. One form from the other.

    Derive the normal form of Green’s theorem, Theorem 3.1.8, from the tangential form, Theorem 3.1.17.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Theorem 3.1.8 is the same theorem in disguise: applying (3.1.6) to the rotated field \(\left\langle -N, M \right\rangle\) turns the circulation into the outward flux and the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of the curl into the divergence. Indeed, for this field the circulation is \(\oint_C (-N)\,dx + M\,dy = \oint_C M\,dy - N\,dx\text{,}\) and its \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of curl is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial (-N)}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y}, \end{equation*}
    so the two sides of the tangential form for \(\left\langle -N, M \right\rangle\) are the two sides of the normal form for \(\mathbf{F}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 3.1.31. The area of an ellipse from a line integral.

    Compute the area of the region \(E\) enclosed by the ellipse \(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\text{,}\) shown in Figure 3.1.30, using the line integral of a vector field around its boundary \(C\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise.
    🔗
    Answer.
    If \(\mathbf{F} = \langle -y,\ 0 \rangle\text{,}\) that is \(M = -y\) and \(N = 0\text{,}\) then \(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 0 - (-1) = 1\text{,}\) and the tangential form of Green’s theorem, (3.1.6), gives
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{T}\, ds\\ \amp \quad = \iint_E \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) dA\\ \amp \quad = \iint_E 1\, dA = \text{area of } E. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Parametrize \(C\) counterclockwise as
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle a\cos t,\ b\sin t \rangle, \qquad 0 \le t \le 2\pi . \end{equation*}
    Then \(\mathbf{v}(t) = \langle -a\sin t,\ b\cos t \rangle\text{,}\) and on \(C\) the field is \(\mathbf{F} = \langle -y,\ 0 \rangle = \langle -b\sin t,\ 0 \rangle\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v} = ab\sin^2 t = \frac{ab}{2}\big(1 - \cos(2t)\big). \end{equation*}
    Since \(\mathbf{T}\,ds = d\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{v}\,dt\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{T}\, ds\\ \amp \quad = \int_{t=0}^{t=2\pi} \frac{ab}{2}\big(1 - \cos(2t)\big)\, dt\\ \amp \quad = \left(\frac{ab}{2}\right)(2\pi) = \pi ab . \end{align*}
    The area of the ellipse is \(\pi ab\text{;}\) when \(a = b = r\) it is a circle, and this is the familiar \(\pi r^2\text{.}\)

    Checkpoint 3.1.32. The centroid from a line integral around the boundary.

    A lamina of constant density \(\delta\) occupies a region \(R\) in the \(xy\)-plane with boundary \(C\text{,}\) oriented counterclockwise. Show that its centroid (Definition 2.8.3) has the coordinates
    \begin{align*} \bar{x} \amp = \frac{1}{2A} \oint_C x^2\, dy \quad \text{and}\\ \bar{y} \amp = -\frac{1}{2A} \oint_C y^2\, dx, \end{align*}
    where \(A\) is the area of the region bounded by \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    Take \(\mathbf{F} = \langle x^2,\ 0 \rangle\text{,}\) so \(M = x^2\) and \(N = 0\text{.}\) The normal form of Green’s theorem, (3.1.4), gives
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}\, ds = \oint_C M\, dy - N\, dx\\ \amp \quad = \iint_R \left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} \right) dA,\\ \amp \oint_C x^2\, dy = \iint_R 2x\, dA = \frac{2M_y}{\delta} \qquad \text{(I)} \end{align*}
    because \(M_y = \iint_R x\,\delta\, dA\) by (2.8.6) and \(\delta\) is constant.
    🔗
    If \(\mathbf{F} = \langle -y^2,\ 0 \rangle\text{,}\) so \(M = -y^2\) and \(N = 0\text{,}\) the tangential form, (3.1.6), gives
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{T}\, ds = \oint_C M\, dx + N\, dy\\ \amp \quad = \iint_R \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) dA,\\ \amp \oint_C -y^2\, dx = \iint_R -(-2y)\, dA\\ \amp \quad = \frac{2M_x}{\delta} \qquad \text{(II)} \end{align*}
    since \(M_x = \iint_R y\,\delta\, dA\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Finally,
    \begin{equation*} \iint_R dA = A = \frac{M}{\delta}. \qquad \text{(III)} \end{equation*}
    By (2.8.7), (I) and (III) give
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M} = \frac{\frac{\delta}{2}\oint_C x^2\, dy}{A\delta} = \frac{1}{2A}\oint_C x^2\, dy, \end{equation*}
    and (II) and (III) give
    \begin{align*} \bar{y} \amp = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{-\frac{\delta}{2}\oint_C y^2\, dx}{A\delta}\\ \amp = -\frac{1}{2A}\oint_C y^2\, dx. \end{align*}
