    Section 2.5 Triple Integrals in Rectangular Coordinates

    Previously, we learned how to calculate double integrals and how to use them to calculate volumes. Today, we will learn triple integrals in Cartesian coordinates, which can also be used to evaluate volumes of solids, along with other applications that we will learn.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.1 What Does a Triple Integral Mean?

    An important application comes first: the mass of a block \(B\) with variable density (mass per unit volume) \(\rho(x,y,z)\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} M = \iiint_B \rho(x,y,z)\,dV.\tag{2.5.1} \end{equation}
    More generally, a triple integral has the form \(\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV\text{,}\) defined by chopping the solid \(E\) into tiny blocks of volume \(\Delta V = \Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\text{,}\) sampling \(f\) in each block, and passing to the limit. A natural question is: what does \(\iiint_E dV\) mean? We can think of it as summing the volumes of the tiny blocks \(\Delta V\text{,}\) hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \text{Volume of } E = \iiint_E dV.\tag{2.5.2} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.2 Case I: \(E = \{(x,y,z) : a \le x \le b,\) \(g_1(x) \le y \le g_2(x),\) \(u_1(x,y) \le z \le u_2(x,y)\}\)

    The first case that we will study is the case where the region of integration is of the form
    🔗
    \begin{align*} E = \{(x,y,z) : \amp a \le x \le b,\ g_1(x) \le y \le g_2(x),\\ \amp u_1(x,y) \le z \le u_2(x,y)\}, \end{align*}
    so \(E\) lies between the surfaces \(z = u_1(x,y)\) and \(z = u_2(x,y)\) over a projection \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane, and
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV = \int_a^b\!\!\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}\!\!\int_{u_1(x,y)}^{u_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx.\tag{2.5.3} \end{equation}

    Example 2.5.1. Example I.

    Calculate the triple integral \(\displaystyle\iiint_E z\,dx\,dy\,dz\text{,}\) where \(E\) is the tetrahedron shown in Figure 2.5.2, bounded by the coordinate planes and the plane \(z = 1 - x - y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let us begin by describing the projection \(D\) (shown in Figure 2.5.3) and the region \(E\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} D \amp= \{(x,y) : 0 \le x \le 1,\ 0 \le y \le -x+1\},\\ E \amp= \{(x,y,z) : 0 \le x \le 1,\ 0 \le y \le -x+1,\ 0 \le z \le 1-x-y\}. \end{align*}
    We then calculate the triple integral as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \iiint_E z\,dV \amp= \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-x}\!\!\int_0^{1-x-y} z\,dz\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-x} \left[\frac{z^2}{2}\right]_{z=0}^{z=1-x-y} dy\,dx\\ \amp= \frac12\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-x}(1-x-y)^2\,dy\,dx\\ \amp= \frac12\int_0^1 \left[-\frac{(1-x-y)^3}{3}\right]_{y=0}^{y=1-x} dx\\ \amp= \frac16\int_0^1 (1-x)^3\,dx = \frac16\left[-\frac{(1-x)^4}{4}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{24}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The role of the projection \(D\) and of the inner \(z\)-integration—a column rising from \(D\) through the solid—is animated in Figure 2.5.4.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.3 Case II: \(E = \{(x,y,z) : h_1(y) \le x \le h_2(y),\) \(c \le y \le d,\) \(u_1(x,y) \le z \le u_2(x,y)\}\)

    The second case is entirely similar to (2.5.3), but the projection \(D\) is described with horizontal strips:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} E = \{(x,y,z) : \amp h_1(y) \le x \le h_2(y),\ c \le y \le d,\\ \amp u_1(x,y) \le z \le u_2(x,y)\}, \end{align*}
    and the corresponding iterated integral is \(\displaystyle\int_c^d\!\!\int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)} \!\!\int_{u_1(x,y)}^{u_2(x,y)} f\,dz\,dx\,dy\text{.}\) In fact, by projecting into the \(xz\)- or \(yz\)-plane instead, a triple integral can be set up in six different orders of integration; the next example illustrates three of them.
    🔗

    Example 2.5.5. Example II.

    Set up the triple integrals below whose value gives the volume of the region \(R\) of Figure 2.5.6, bounded by the cylinder \(z = 1 - x^2\text{,}\) the plane \(y = 1 - x\text{,}\) and the coordinate planes. Then, evaluate two out of the three integrals.
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle \iiint_R dz\,dx\,dy\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \iiint_R dy\,dx\,dz\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle \iiint_R dx\,dy\,dz\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Question: How many more triple integrals can you think of for computing such a volume? (Three more; six in total.)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1. Part A
    For the order \(dz\,dx\,dy\text{,}\) we project into the \(xy\)-plane (the triangle on the left in Figure 2.5.7): \(0 \le y \le 1\text{,}\) \(0 \le x \le 1-y\text{,}\) and the 3D figure gives \(0 \le z \le 1 - x^2\text{.}\) Hence
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \int_{y=0}^{1}\!\!\int_{x=0}^{1-y}\!\!\int_{z=0}^{1-x^2} dz\,dx\,dy \amp= \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-y}\left(1 - x^2\right)dx\,dy\\ \amp= \int_0^1\left[x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{x=0}^{x=1-y} dy\\ \amp= \int_0^1\left[(1-y) - \frac{(1-y)^3}{3}\right]dy\\ \amp= \left[y - \frac{y^2}{2} + \frac{(1-y)^4}{12}\right]_0^1\\ \amp= \left(1 - \frac12\right) - \frac{1}{12} = \frac{5}{12}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution 2. Part B
    For the order \(dy\,dx\,dz\text{,}\) we project into the \(xz\)-plane (the middle region in Figure 2.5.7): \(0 \le z \le 1\text{,}\) \(0 \le x \le \sqrt{1-z}\text{,}\) and the 3D figure gives the limits of the last variable: \(0 \le y \le 1-x\text{.}\) Hence
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \int_{z=0}^{1}\!\!\int_{x=0}^{\sqrt{1-z}}\!\!\int_{y=0}^{1-x} dy\,dx\,dz \amp= \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{\sqrt{1-z}}(1-x)\,dx\,dz\\ \amp= \int_0^1\left[x - \frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{x=0}^{x=\sqrt{1-z}} dz\\ \amp= \int_0^1\left[(1-z)^{1/2} - \tfrac12(1-z)\right]dz\\ \amp= \left[-\tfrac23(1-z)^{3/2} + \tfrac14(1-z)^2\right]_0^1\\ \amp= 0 - \left(-\frac23 + \frac14\right) = \frac{5}{12}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution 3. Part C
    For the order \(dx\,dy\,dz\text{,}\) we project into the \(yz\)-plane (the region on the right in Figure 2.5.7). This time the projection must be split by the curve \(z = 1-(1-y)^2\) into
    🔗
    \begin{align*} R_1 \amp= \{(y,z) : 0 \le y \le 1,\ 0 \le z \le 1-(1-y)^2\},\\ R_2 \amp= \{(y,z) : 0 \le y \le 1,\ 1-(1-y)^2 \le z \le 1\}, \end{align*}
    with \(0 \le x \le 1-y\) on \(R_1\) (the plane is reached first) and \(0 \le x \le \sqrt{1-z}\) on \(R_2\) (the cylinder is reached first). Hence
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \iiint_R dx\,dy\,dz \amp= \int_{y=0}^{1}\!\!\int_{z=0}^{1-(1-y)^2} \!\!\int_{x=0}^{1-y} dx\,dz\,dy\\ \amp\quad + \int_{y=0}^{1}\!\!\int_{z=1-(1-y)^2}^{1} \!\!\int_{x=0}^{\sqrt{1-z}} dx\,dz\,dy\\ \amp= \frac14 + \frac16 = \frac{5}{12}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.4 Mass With Variable Density

    Example 2.5.9. Example III.

    Assume that the density (mass per unit volume) is given by \(\rho(x,y,z) = x\text{.}\) Evaluate the mass \(M\) of the solid \(R\) of Figure 2.5.6.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Using the mass formula (2.5.1) with the same limits as in Part A of Example 2.5.5:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M \amp= \int_{y=0}^{1}\!\!\int_{x=0}^{1-y}\!\!\int_{z=0}^{1-x^2} x\,dz\,dx\,dy = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-y} x\left(1 - x^2\right)dx\,dy\\ \amp= \int_0^1\left[\frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{4}\right]_{x=0}^{x=1-y} dy = \int_0^1\left[\frac{(1-y)^2}{2} - \frac{(1-y)^4}{4}\right]dy\\ \amp= \left[-\frac{(1-y)^3}{6} + \frac{(1-y)^5}{20}\right]_0^1 = \frac16 - \frac1{20} = \frac{7}{60}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.5.5 Concept Check

    The first five questions all concern the same solid \(E\text{,}\) the region bounded below by the paraboloid \(z = x^2+y^2\) and above by the upper half \(z = 1+\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}\) of the sphere of radius \(1\) centered at \((0,0,1)\text{,}\) drawn in Figure 2.5.15. Four of them ask for a sign or a yes-or-no answer rather than a number: the point is to read the integrand on the region, not to integrate.
    🔗
    It is worth locating \(E\) first. The two surfaces meet where \(x^2+y^2 = 1+\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}\text{;}\) writing \(u = x^2+y^2\) this is \(u-1 = \sqrt{1-u}\text{,}\) hence \(u^2 = u\) and \(u = 1\text{.}\) So \(E\) sits over the unit disk \(x^2+y^2 \le 1\text{,}\) and for each \((x,y)\) there, \(z\) runs from \(x^2+y^2\) up to \(1+\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.10. The sign of an integral, without calculating.

    Without calculation, decide whether the integral below is positive, negative, or zero. The region of integration \(E\) is the region bounded by \(z=x^2+y^2\) and \(z=1+\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}\text{.}\) See Figure 2.5.15.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_E \left(z-\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}-1\right) dV \end{equation*}
    1. Positive
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Negative
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Zero
      🔗
      🔗
    Hint: begin by determining the limits of integration.
    🔗
    Answer.
    B, negative.
    🔗
    The upper surface of \(E\) is \(z = 1+\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}\text{,}\) so every point of \(E\) satisfies \(z \le 1+\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}\text{,}\) that is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z-\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}-1 \le 0 . \end{equation*}
    The integrand is exactly the (signed) vertical distance from a point of \(E\) up to the sphere, so it is never positive, and it vanishes only on the cap itself, a surface of zero volume. An integrand that is negative on the interior of the solid gives a negative integral.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.11. Could it be a mass density?

    Can the function \(f(x,y,z)=z-\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}-1\) represent mass density (mass per unit volume) over the region \(E\text{,}\) which is graphed in Figure 2.5.15?
    🔗
    1. Yes
      🔗
      🔗
    2. No
      🔗
      🔗
    Answer.
    B, no. Mass is never negative, so a mass density must satisfy \(f \ge 0\text{.}\) As we just saw, this \(f\) is negative throughout the interior of \(E\text{.}\) A region carrying it would have negative mass.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.12. Could it be a charge density?

    Can the function \(f(x,y,z)=z-\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}-1\) represent charge density (charge per unit volume) over the region \(E\text{,}\) which is graphed in Figure 2.5.15?
    🔗
    1. Yes
      🔗
      🔗
    2. No
      🔗
      🔗
    Answer.
    A, yes. Charge comes in both signs, so a charge density is free to be negative; there is nothing wrong with a body that is negatively charged everywhere. The triple integral of Checkpoint 2.5.10 is then the total charge, and its negative sign says the body carries a net negative charge.
    🔗
    This is the whole difference between the two questions: the mathematics is identical, and only the physical meaning of the sign changes.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.13. The sign of a second integral.

    Without calculation, decide whether the integral below is positive, negative, or zero. The region of integration \(E\) is the region bounded by \(z=x^2+y^2\) and \(z=1+\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}\text{.}\) See Figure 2.5.15.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iiint_E \left(2-x^2-y^2\right) dV \end{equation*}
    1. Positive
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Negative
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Zero
      🔗
      🔗
    Answer.
    A, positive.
    🔗
    The solid lies over the unit disk, so \(x^2+y^2 \le 1\) at every point of \(E\text{,}\) and therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2-x^2-y^2 \ge 2-1 = 1 \gt 0 . \end{equation*}
    The integrand is at least \(1\) on all of \(E\text{,}\) so the integral is positive—in fact it is at least the volume of \(E\text{.}\) Note that no antiderivative was needed: only the projection of \(E\) onto the \(xy\)-plane.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.14. Could this one be a mass density?

    Can the function \(f(x,y,z)=2-x^2-y^2\) represent mass density (mass per unit volume) over the region \(E\text{,}\) which is graphed in Figure 2.5.15?
    🔗
    1. Yes
      🔗
      🔗
    2. No
      🔗
      🔗
    Answer.
    A, yes. On \(E\) we have \(1 \le 2-x^2-y^2 \le 2\text{,}\) so \(f\) is positive there and can be read as a density: the solid is densest along its axis and lightest near the rim, where the two surfaces meet. Its mass would be the integral of Checkpoint 2.5.13.
    🔗
    Beware of the phrase “over the region \(E\)”. The same formula would not do on a larger region: outside the cylinder \(x^2+y^2 = 2\) it turns negative.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.5.16. A volume between two cylinders.

    Set up a triple integral for computing the volume of the region bounded by the two cylinders \(y=x^2\) and \(y=\sqrt{x}\) and the two planes \(z=0\) and \(z=\frac13(x+y+1)\text{.}\) See Figure 2.5.17.
    🔗
    Answer.
    The two cylinders are vertical walls standing on the curves \(y=x^2\) and \(y=\sqrt{x}\) of the \(xy\)-plane, and those curves cross at \(x=0\) and \(x=1\text{.}\) Between them \(\sqrt{x} \ge x^2\text{,}\) so the shadow of the solid is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} D = \left\{(x,y) : 0 \le x \le 1,\ x^2 \le y \le \sqrt{x}\right\} , \end{equation*}
    which is the region of Figure 2.5.17 lying between the two walls. The floor is \(z=0\) and the ceiling is the plane, so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}}\!\!\int_{0}^{\frac13(x+y+1)} dz\,dy\,dx . \end{equation*}
    Carrying it out is quick, since the innermost integral just returns the height of the ceiling:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = \frac13\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} (x+y+1)\,dy\,dx = \frac{19}{90} \approx 0.211 . \end{equation*}
