Theorem 3.1.17. Green’s Theorem (Circulation-Curl or Tangential Form).

Let \(C\) be a piecewise smooth, simple closed curve enclosing a region \(R\) in the plane. Let \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\) be a vector field with \(M\) and \(N\) having continuous first partial derivatives in an open region containing \(R\text{.}\) Then the counterclockwise circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around \(C\) equals the double integral of \((\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{k}\) over \(R\text{:}\)
🔗
\begin{align} \amp \underbrace{\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{T}\, ds}_{\text{circulation}} = \oint_C M\, dx + N\, dy\notag\\ \amp \quad = \underbrace{\iint_R \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy}_{\substack{\text{integral of} \\ \operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{k} \text{ over } R}}.\tag{3.1.6} \end{align}
🔗

Proof.
Write \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\text{.}\) Both sides of (3.1.6) split into two pieces, one carrying \(M\) and one carrying \(N\text{,}\) so it is enough to prove the two separate statements
🔗
\begin{align} \oint_C M\,dx \amp = -\iint_R \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\,dA ,\tag{3.1.7}\\ \oint_C N\,dy \amp = \iint_R \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\,dA ,\notag \end{align}
and add them. Note the minus sign in the first: it is the whole reason the curl carries one.
🔗
Step 1: the \(M\) statement, on a region cut by vertical lines. Suppose first that \(R\) is a region of the kind studied in Subsection 2.2.1,
🔗
\begin{align*} R = \bigl\{ (x,y) : \amp\ a \le x \le b,\\ \amp\ f_2(x) \le y \le f_1(x) \bigr\} , \end{align*}
so that \(C\) is made of the bottom curve \(y = f_2(x)\) travelled left to right, the top curve \(y = f_1(x)\) travelled right to left, and possibly two vertical segments at \(x=a\) and \(x=b\text{.}\) This is the picture in Figure 3.1.18. On the vertical segments \(x\) never changes, so \(dx = 0\) and they contribute nothing to \(\oint_C M\,dx\text{.}\) What is left is
🔗
\begin{align*} \amp \oint_C M\,dx\\ \amp = \int_a^b M\bigl(x,f_2(x)\bigr)\,dx\\ \amp \quad {} + \int_b^a M\bigl(x,f_1(x)\bigr)\,dx\\ \amp = -\int_a^b \Bigl[M\bigl(x,f_1(x)\bigr)\\ \amp \qquad {} - M\bigl(x,f_2(x)\bigr)\Bigr] dx , \end{align*}
the sign appearing because the top is travelled backwards. Meanwhile, doing the double integral in the \(y\)-direction first and applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to the inner integral,
🔗
\begin{align*} \amp \iint_R \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\,dA\\ \amp = \int_a^b\left[\int_{f_2(x)}^{f_1(x)} \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\,dy\right] dx\\ \amp = \int_a^b \Bigl[M\bigl(x,f_1(x)\bigr) - M\bigl(x,f_2(x)\bigr)\Bigr] dx . \end{align*}
The two agree up to the minus sign, which is the first line of (3.1.7).
🔗
Step 2: the \(N\) statement. Now read \(R\) the other way, as in Subsection 2.2.2, with \(c \le y \le d\) and \(g_1(y) \le x \le g_2(y)\text{.}\) On the horizontal parts of the boundary \(dy = 0\text{,}\) and the same computation with the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) exchanged gives the second line of (3.1.7). Here the right-hand curve is travelled upwards, in the direction of increasing \(y\text{,}\) which is why no minus sign appears. Adding the two lines proves the theorem for a region that can be described both ways.
🔗
Step 3: general regions. A region that cannot be described both ways at once is cut into finitely many pieces that can, exactly as in Subsection 2.2.1, and the theorem is applied to each piece. Each cut is travelled twice, once for the piece on either side, and in opposite directions, so those two line integrals cancel. What survives is the integral around the original curve \(C\text{,}\) while the double integrals add up to the integral over \(R\text{.}\)
