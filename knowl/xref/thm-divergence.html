Theorem 3.3.5. Gauss’s Divergence Theorem.

Let \(\mathbf{F}\) be a vector field whose components have continuous first partial derivatives, and let \(S\) be a piecewise smooth oriented closed surface. The flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across \(S\) in the direction of the surface’s outward unit normal field \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) equals the integral of \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\) over the region \(D\) enclosed by the surface:
🔗
\begin{equation} \underbrace{\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma}_{\text{outward flux through the boundary } S} = \underbrace{\iiint_D \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\, dV}_{\text{triple integral of the divergence over } D}.\tag{3.3.2} \end{equation}
🔗

Proof.
Write \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j} + P\,\mathbf{k}\text{.}\) Both sides of (3.3.2) break into three pieces along the three components: by (3.3.1) the right-hand side is the sum of the integrals of \(\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial x}\text{,}\) \(\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial y}\) and \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial z}\text{,}\) and the left-hand side is the sum of the fluxes of \(M\,\mathbf{i}\text{,}\) \(N\,\mathbf{j}\) and \(P\,\mathbf{k}\text{.}\) So it is enough to prove the three separate statements
🔗
\begin{align} \iint_S M\,\mathbf{i} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iiint_D \frac{\partial M}{\partial x}\, dV ,\tag{3.3.3}\\ \iint_S N\,\mathbf{j} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iiint_D \frac{\partial N}{\partial y}\, dV ,\notag\\ \iint_S P\,\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iiint_D \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\, dV ,\notag \end{align}
and add them. We prove the third; the other two are the same argument with the axes renamed.
🔗
Step 1: a solid that is simple in the \(z\)-direction. Suppose first that \(D\) can be described as in Subsection 2.5.2, that is
🔗
\begin{equation*} D = \left\{(x,y,z) : (x,y) \in R,\ u_1(x,y) \le z \le u_2(x,y)\right\} , \end{equation*}
where \(R\) is the shadow of \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane and \(u_1 \le u_2\) are smooth. Its boundary \(S\) then falls into three pieces, drawn in Figure 3.3.6: the cap \(S_2\) where \(z = u_2(x,y)\text{,}\) the floor \(S_1\) where \(z = u_1(x,y)\text{,}\) and the vertical wall \(S_3\) standing over the boundary of \(R\text{.}\)
🔗
On the wall the outward normal is horizontal, so \(\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = 0\) and \(S_3\) contributes nothing. On the cap the outward normal points upward, and for a graph \(z = u_2(x,y)\) we already know from (2.10.6) that \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \left\langle -\dfrac{\partial u_2}{\partial x},\ -\dfrac{\partial u_2}{\partial y},\ 1 \right\rangle dx\, dy\text{,}\) so that \(\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = dx\, dy\text{.}\) On the floor the outward normal points downward, which flips the sign, and \(\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = -dx\, dy\text{.}\) Hence
🔗
\begin{align*} \iint_S P\,\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iint_{S_2} P\,\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma + \iint_{S_1} P\,\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma\\ \amp = \iint_R P\bigl(x,y,u_2(x,y)\bigr) dx\, dy\\ \amp \qquad - \iint_R P\bigl(x,y,u_1(x,y)\bigr) dx\, dy . \end{align*}
Step 2: the triple integral. Integrating first in \(z\text{,}\) as in (2.5.3), and using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus on the inner integral,
🔗
\begin{align*} \iiint_D \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\, dV \amp = \iint_R \left[\int_{u_1(x,y)}^{u_2(x,y)} \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\, dz\right] dx\, dy\\ \amp = \iint_R \Bigl[P\bigl(x,y,u_2(x,y)\bigr) - P\bigl(x,y,u_1(x,y)\bigr)\Bigr] dx\, dy , \end{align*}
which is exactly what Step 1 produced. So the third statement of (3.3.3) holds for such a solid. Running the same argument with \(D\) viewed as simple in the \(x\)-direction gives the first statement, and in the \(y\)-direction the second. Adding the three proves the theorem for any solid that is simple in all three directions.
🔗
Step 3: general solids. A solid that is not simple in all three directions is cut into finitely many pieces that are, exactly as a region of the plane was cut in Subsection 2.2.1. Apply the theorem to each piece and add. Every cut surface is shared by two neighbouring pieces, and it carries opposite outward normals as seen from the two sides, so the two flux contributions differ only in sign and cancel. What survives on the left is the flux through the original boundary \(S\text{,}\) and the volume integrals simply add up to the integral over \(D\text{.}\) This is the same bookkeeping that made the loop property of Section 1.5 work, one dimension up.
