    Section 2.6 Triple Integrals in Cylindrical Coordinates

    Previously, we learned how to calculate triple integrals in rectangular coordinates. Today, we will learn triple integrals in cylindrical coordinates, which can be used to evaluate volumes of solids or to integrate a function over a region in space. We will see that when there is symmetry about an axis, it is more convenient to use cylindrical coordinates to calculate a triple integral.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.6.1 Cylindrical Coordinates

    Cylindrical coordinates \((r,\theta,z)\) are the combination of polar coordinates \((r,\theta)\) in the \(xy\)-plane and the \(z\)-coordinate of Cartesian coordinates; see Figure 2.6.1. Thus, adding \(z\) to (2.3.1), the relations between Cartesian and cylindrical coordinates are
    🔗
    \begin{equation} x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad z = z,\tag{2.6.1} \end{equation}
    \begin{equation*} r^2 = x^2 + y^2, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}, \qquad z = z. \end{equation*}
    Before calculating triple integrals in cylindrical coordinates, we need to figure out the volume element \(dV\) in this new set of coordinates. Remember from (2.4.1) that the area element \(dA\) in polar coordinates is \(r\,dr\,d\theta\text{;}\) stacking it through a height \(dz\) gives the cylindrical volume element
    🔗
    \begin{equation} dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.\tag{2.6.2} \end{equation}
    

    Instructions.
    Use the three sliders to build the volume element one edge at a time. The \(dr\) slider sets the radial thickness of the block, the \(d\theta\) slider opens its angular width, and the \(dz\) slider raises its height. Start with all three small, then increase them in turn. The edges controlled by \(dr\) and \(dz\) are straight segments of exactly those lengths, but the edge swept out by \(d\theta\) is a circular arc of length \(r\,d\theta\)—it grows in proportion to the block’s distance \(r\) from the \(z\)-axis. Slide \(d\theta\) alone and watch that curved edge lengthen while the radial and vertical edges stay fixed.
    🔗
    Up close the block is essentially a rectangular box with edge lengths \(dr\text{,}\) \(r\,d\theta\text{,}\) and \(dz\text{,}\) so multiplying the three edges gives:
    \begin{equation*} dV = dr \cdot r\,d\theta \cdot dz = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Now suppose our goal is to integrate over a region \(E\) described as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} E = \left\{(x,y,z)\ \middle|\ (x,y)\in D,\ u_1(x,y) \le z \le u_2(x,y)\right\}, \end{equation*}
    where the region \(D\) is known in polar coordinates:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} D = \left\{(r,\theta)\ \middle|\ \alpha\le\theta\le\beta,\ h_1(\theta) \le r \le h_2(\theta)\right\}. \end{equation*}
    Then the iterated integral (2.5.3), written as \(\iiint_E f\,dV = \iint_D\big[\int_{u_1}^{u_2} f\,dz\big]dA\text{,}\) can be converted to cylindrical coordinates:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\!\!\int_{h_1(\theta)}^{h_2(\theta)} \!\!\int_{u_1(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta)}^{u_2(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta)} f(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta,\ z)\ r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta.\tag{2.6.3} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Subsection 2.6.2 The Mass of a Solid With Axial Symmetry

    Example 2.6.2. Example I.

    A solid \(E\) lies within the cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\text{,}\) below the plane \(z = 4\text{,}\) and above the paraboloid \(z = 1 - x^2 - y^2\text{.}\) Given that the density of the solid at any point is proportional to its distance from the axis of the cylinder, find the mass of the solid.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let us begin by plotting the region of integration (Figure 2.6.3). Note that the equation of the cylinder in cylindrical coordinates is \(r = 1\text{,}\) and the equation of the paraboloid is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z = 1 - x^2 - y^2 = 1 - \left(r^2\cos^2\theta + r^2\sin^2\theta\right) = 1 - r^2. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the limits of integration can be written as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} E = \left\{(r,\theta,z)\ \middle|\ 0\le\theta\le 2\pi,\ 0 \le r \le 1,\ 1 - r^2 \le z \le 4\right\}, \end{equation*}
    since the projection into the \(xy\)-plane is a circle of radius \(1\) and the height of the solid is bounded between the paraboloid \(z = 1 - r^2\) and \(z = 4\text{.}\) Since we know that the density \(\delta(x,y,z)\) of the solid is proportional to the distance from the axis of the cylinder, we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \delta(x,y,z) = K\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \end{equation*}
    and since \(r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\text{,}\) in cylindrical coordinates \(\delta(r,\theta,z) = Kr\text{.}\) Thus, by (2.5.1) and (2.6.3), we can compute the mass of the solid as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} m = \iiint_E K\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\,dV \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_{1-r^2}^{4} (Kr)\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1 Kr^2\left[4 - \left(1 - r^2\right)\right]dr\,d\theta\\ \amp= K\int_0^{2\pi}\!d\theta\int_0^1\left(3r^2 + r^4\right)dr\\ \amp= 2\pi K\left[r^3 + \frac{r^5}{5}\right]_0^1 = \frac{12\pi K}{5}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The way a column of the solid runs from the paraboloid floor up to the plane \(z = 4\text{,}\) and how it sweeps around the axis, is animated in Figure 2.6.4.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.6.3 Concept Check

    Each part below describes a solid by the surfaces that bound it. Set up a triple integral in cylindrical coordinates that gives its volume: the work is all in the limits, and (2.6.2) supplies the rest. Try each part from the description alone—the solution shows the solid, so you can check whether the picture in your head was the right one.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.6.5. Eight volumes in cylindrical coordinates.

    Set up triple integrals, in cylindrical coordinates, which give the volume of each of the following regions.
    🔗

    (a)

    The volume of the region bounded above by the paraboloid \(z = 5-x^2-y^2\text{,}\) below by the plane \(z=0\text{,}\) and on the side by the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The bounding surfaces are the ones drawn in Figure 2.6.6, and in cylindrical coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2}\!\!\int_0^{\,5-r^2} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_0^2 \left(5r - r^3\right)dr = 12\pi . \end{equation*}
    The cylinder is \(r=2\) and the paraboloid is \(z = 5-r^2\text{;}\) they meet at \(z=1\text{,}\) which is where the wall of the cylinder stops.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    The volume of the region bounded below by the paraboloid \(z = \frac14\left(x^2+y^2\right)\) and above by the plane \(z=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The bounding surfaces are the ones drawn in Figure 2.6.7, and in cylindrical coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2}\!\!\int_{r^2/4}^{1} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_0^2 \left(r - \frac{r^3}{4}\right)dr = 2\pi . \end{equation*}
    The paraboloid is \(z = r^2/4\text{,}\) and it meets \(z=1\) at \(r=2\text{,}\) which fixes the outer limit of \(r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    The volume of the region bounded above by the paraboloid \(z = \frac14\left(x^2+y^2\right)\text{,}\) below by the plane \(z=0\text{,}\) and on the side by the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The bounding surfaces are the ones drawn in Figure 2.6.8, and in cylindrical coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2}\!\!\int_0^{\,r^2/4} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_0^2 \frac{r^3}{4}\,dr = 2\pi . \end{equation*}
    This solid and the one of Task 2.6.5.b together fill the cylinder \(r \le 2\text{,}\) \(0 \le z \le 1\text{,}\) whose volume is \(4\pi\)—which is why both answers are \(2\pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    The volume of the region bounded below by the lower half of the sphere \(x^2+y^2+(z-1)^2=4\) and above by the plane \(z=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The bounding surfaces are the ones drawn in Figure 2.6.9, and in cylindrical coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2}\!\!\int_{1-\sqrt{4-r^2}}^{1} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_0^2 r\sqrt{4-r^2}\,dr = \frac{16\pi}{3} . \end{equation*}
    The sphere has center \((0,0,1)\) and radius \(2\text{,}\) so its lower half is \(z = 1-\sqrt{4-r^2}\) and the answer is the volume \(\frac23\pi(2)^3\) of a hemisphere of radius \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    The volume of the region bounded by the cone \(z^2 = \frac14\left(x^2+y^2\right)\) and the planes \(z=1\) and \(z=-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The bounding surfaces are the ones drawn in Figure 2.6.10, and in cylindrical coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = 2\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2}\!\!\int_{r/2}^{1} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = 4\pi\int_0^2 \left(r - \frac{r^2}{2}\right)dr = \frac{8\pi}{3} . \end{equation*}
    The cone is \(z = \pm r/2\text{,}\) and a point lies inside its upper half when \(z \ge r/2\text{.}\) The solid is two cones of radius \(2\) and height \(1\text{,}\) each of volume \(\frac13\pi(2)^2(1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    The volume of the region bounded by the hyperboloid of one sheet \(x^2+y^2-z^2=4\) and the planes \(z=3\) and \(z=-3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The bounding surfaces are the ones drawn in Figure 2.6.11, and in cylindrical coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{-3}^{3}\!\!\int_0^{\sqrt{z^2+4}} r\,dr\,dz\,d\theta = \pi\int_{-3}^{3}\left(z^2+4\right)dz = 42\pi . \end{equation*}
    Here it pays to integrate \(r\) first: the wall is \(r = \sqrt{z^2+4}\text{,}\) one formula for every \(z\text{,}\) while slicing the other way would take two pieces. The waist has radius \(2\) and the caps radius \(\sqrt{13}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    The volume of the region bounded by the cone \(z^2 = \frac14\left(x^2+y^2\right)\) and the planes \(z=2\) and \(z=-1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The bounding surfaces are the ones drawn in Figure 2.6.12, and in cylindrical coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{0}^{2}\!\!\int_0^{2z} r\,dr\,dz\,d\theta + \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{-1}^{0}\!\!\int_0^{-2z} r\,dr\,dz\,d\theta = 12\pi . \end{equation*}
    The same cone as in Task 2.6.5.e, cut at different heights: a piece of radius \(4\) and height \(2\) and one of radius \(2\) and height \(1\text{,}\) of volumes \(\frac{32\pi}{3}\) and \(\frac{4\pi}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (h)

    The volume of the region bounded above by the cone \(z = 5-\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\text{,}\) below by the plane \(z=0\text{,}\) and on the side by the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The bounding surfaces are the ones drawn in Figure 2.6.13, and in cylindrical coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{2}\!\!\int_0^{\,5-r} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\int_0^2 \left(5r - r^2\right)dr = \frac{44\pi}{3} . \end{equation*}
    The cone is \(z = 5-r\text{,}\) which is \(3\) at the wall \(r=2\text{.}\) Compare Task 2.6.5.a: the same picture with \(5-r\) in place of \(5-r^2\text{.}\)
