    Section 2.9 Substitution in Multiple Integrals and the Jacobian Determinant

    In single-variable calculus, substitution converts a difficult integral into an easier one at the cost of a correction factor \(dx/du\text{.}\) In this section we develop the analogous tool for double and triple integrals. The correction factor is now a determinant—the Jacobian determinant—which measures how a change of variables stretches or shrinks area and volume.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.9.1 The Jacobian Determinant

    Assume that there exists a transformation \(T\) that relates two coordinate systems, \((u,v)\) and \((x,y)\) coordinates. In other words, there exist two functions \(f\) and \(g\) such that \(x = f(u,v)\) and \(y = g(u,v)\text{.}\) As illustrated in Figure 2.9.1, the region \(S\) in the \(uv\)-plane is transformed to the region \(R\) in the \(xy\)-plane, and the inverse transformation \(T^{-1}\) carries \(R\) back to \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 2.9.2.

    The Jacobian determinant of the transformation \(x = f(u,v)\text{,}\) \(y = g(u,v)\) is defined as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} J(u,v) = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \begin{vmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} \amp \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\[2ex] \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} \amp \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v} \end{vmatrix} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}.\tag{2.9.1} \end{equation}
    In 3D, this definition is generalized to
    🔗
    \begin{equation} J(u,v,w) = \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} = \begin{vmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} \amp \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} \amp \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial w} \\[2ex] \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} \amp \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v} \amp \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial w} \\[2ex] \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial u} \amp \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial v} \amp \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial w} \end{vmatrix}.\tag{2.9.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Then the multiple integrals can be converted from one coordinate system to another as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \iint_R F(x,y)\, dx\, dy = \iint_S F\big(f(u,v),\, g(u,v)\big) \left| \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \right| du\, dv,\tag{2.9.3} \end{equation}
    and in three dimensions, with \(x = f(u,v,w)\text{,}\) \(y = g(u,v,w)\text{,}\) \(z = h(u,v,w)\) carrying a region \(E\) in \(uvw\)-space onto a region \(D\) in \(xyz\)-space,
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \iiint_D F(x,y,z)\, dx\, dy\, dz = \iiint_E F\big(f(u,v,w),\, g(u,v,w),\, h(u,v,w)\big) \left| \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} \right| du\, dv\, dw.\tag{2.9.4} \end{equation}
    The integrals (2.9.3) and (2.9.4) should be compared to the single-variable substitution \(x = h(u)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{x=h(a)}^{x=h(b)} f(x)\, dx = \int_{u=a}^{u=b} f(h(u))\, \frac{dx}{du}\, du. \end{equation*}
    Note that in the past we used to use the substitution of the form \(u = g(x)\) and write:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{x=a}^{x=b} f(g(x))\, \frac{du}{dx}\, dx = \int_{u=g(a)}^{u=g(b)} f(u)\, du. \end{equation*}
    The derivative factor \(dx/du\) records how much the substitution stretches lengths. In two variables, the Jacobian determinant plays the same role for areas: \(|J|\) is the local ratio of area in the \(xy\)-plane to area in the \(uv\)-plane. The next example makes this ratio concrete.
    🔗

    Example 2.9.4. A First Substitution: Scaling a Rectangle.

    Use the transformation \(u = 3x\text{,}\) \(v = 2y\) to evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{y=0}^{1} \int_{x=0}^{1} 6xy\, dx\, dy, \end{equation*}
    and verify that the Jacobian determinant equals the ratio of the areas of the two regions of integration.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The transformation is \(T(u,v) = (x,y) = \left( \tfrac{u}{3}, \tfrac{v}{2} \right)\text{,}\) which carries the rectangle \(S = [0,3] \times [0,2]\) in the \(uv\)-plane onto the unit square \(R = [0,1] \times [0,1]\) in the \(xy\)-plane, as shown in Figure 2.9.6. By (2.9.1), the Jacobian determinant is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \begin{vmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp \frac{1}{2} \end{vmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}, \end{equation*}
    which is exactly the ratio of the areas, \(\dfrac{A_R}{A_S} = \dfrac{1}{6}\text{:}\) the map shrinks areas by the factor \(6\text{,}\) uniformly. Substituting \(x = u/3\text{,}\) \(y = v/2\text{,}\) and \(dx\, dy = \tfrac{1}{6}\, du\, dv\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \int_0^1 \int_0^1 6xy\, dx\, dy \amp= \int_{v=0}^{2} \int_{u=0}^{3} 6 \left( \frac{u}{3} \right) \left( \frac{v}{2} \right) \left( \frac{1}{3} \right) \left( \frac{1}{2} \right) du\, dv\\ \amp= \frac{1}{6} \int_0^2 \int_0^3 uv\, du\, dv = \frac{1}{6} \left( \frac{9}{2} \right) (2) = \frac{3}{2}, \end{align*}
    which agrees with computing the integral directly: \(\int_0^1 \int_0^1 6xy\, dx\, dy = 6 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{3}{2}\text{.}\) The animation in Figure 2.9.5 shows how the Jacobian acts as an area conversion factor.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.9.2 Choosing a Transformation

    In practice we usually run the process in the other direction: the region of integration \(R\) is awkward in \(xy\)-coordinates, and we look for a substitution under which it corresponds to a simple region—ideally a rectangle—with constant limits of integration. The boundary curves of \(R\) usually tell us what \(u\) and \(v\) should be.
    🔗

    Example 2.9.7. A Parallelogram Region.

    Use the transformation \(T(u,v) = (u - 2v,\, v)\) to compute
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_D (x + 3y)\, dx\, dy, \end{equation*}
    where \(D\) is the parallelogram bounded by the lines \(x + 2y = 6\text{,}\) \(x + 2y = 10\text{,}\) \(y = 1\text{,}\) and \(y = 3\text{,}\) shown in Figure 2.9.9.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The transformation gives \(x = u - 2v\) and \(y = v\text{,}\) so by (2.9.1),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp -2 \\ 0 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1. \end{equation*}
    Inverting the transformation, \(u = x + 2y\) and \(v = y\text{.}\) Therefore the boundary lines \(x + 2y = 6\) and \(x + 2y = 10\) become the vertical lines \(u = 6\) and \(u = 10\text{,}\) while \(y = 1\) and \(y = 3\) become \(v = 1\) and \(v = 3\text{:}\) the parallelogram \(D\) corresponds to the rectangle
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R = \{ (u,v) : 6 \le u \le 10,\ 1 \le v \le 3 \}. \end{equation*}
    The integrand becomes \(x + 3y = (u - 2v) + 3v = u + v\text{,}\) so by (2.9.3),
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \iint_D (x + 3y)\, dx\, dy \amp= \int_1^3 \int_6^{10} (u + v)\, \lvert 1 \rvert\, du\, dv = \int_1^3 \left[ \frac{u^2}{2} + uv \right]_{u=6}^{u=10} dv\\ \amp= \int_1^3 (32 + 4v)\, dv = \Big[ 32v + 2v^2 \Big]_1^3 = 64 + 16 = 80. \end{align*}
    Watch the region unfold into a rectangle in Figure 2.9.8.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.9.10. A Curved Region Mapped to a Rectangle.

    Let \(D\) be the region in the first quadrant bounded by the hyperbolas \(x^2 - y^2 = -3\text{,}\) \(x^2 - y^2 = 3\text{,}\) \(xy = 1\text{,}\) and \(xy = 4\text{,}\) shown in Figure 2.9.13.
    🔗
    1. Find a transformation \(T\) that relates \(D\) to a rectangle \(R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Compute the integral \(\displaystyle \iint_D xy\, (x^2 + y^2)\, dx\, dy\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    Part A. The boundary curves suggest the substitution
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u = x^2 - y^2, \qquad v = xy, \end{equation*}
    since then the four boundary curves become the four lines \(u = -3\text{,}\) \(u = 3\text{,}\) \(v = 1\text{,}\) and \(v = 4\text{.}\) Under this substitution the curved region \(D\) corresponds to the rectangle
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R = \{ (u,v) : -3 \le u \le 3,\ 1 \le v \le 4 \}. \end{equation*}
    Part B. Here the substitution is given in the direction \(u = u(x,y)\text{,}\) \(v = v(x,y)\text{,}\) so it is easier to first compute the Jacobian of \((u,v)\) with respect to \((x,y)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)} = \begin{vmatrix} 2x \amp -2y \\ y \amp x \end{vmatrix} = 2x^2 + 2y^2 = 2(x^2 + y^2). \end{equation*}
    According to the inverse function theorem, the Jacobian we need is the reciprocal of this one:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \left( \frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)} \right)^{-1} = \frac{1}{2(x^2 + y^2)}.\tag{2.9.5} \end{equation}
    Now apply (2.9.3). The factor \(x^2 + y^2\) in the integrand cancels against the Jacobian, and \(xy = v\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \iint_D xy\,(x^2 + y^2)\, dx\, dy \amp= \iint_R v\, (x^2 + y^2) \cdot \frac{1}{2(x^2 + y^2)}\, du\, dv\\ \amp= \frac{1}{2} \int_{-3}^{3} \int_1^4 v\, dv\, du = \frac{1}{2}\, (6) \left( \frac{15}{2} \right) = \frac{45}{2}. \end{align*}
    Without the substitution, even describing \(D\) with explicit \(xy\)-limits would be painful; with it, the integral collapses in two lines. See Figure 2.9.11 for the animation, and Figure 2.9.12 to watch \(|J|\) appear as the local ratio of areas.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.9.3 Substitution in Triple Integrals

    Example 2.9.14. Volume of a Parallelepiped by Substitution.

    Find the volume of the parallelepiped shown in Figure 2.9.17 by writing the equations of all faces of the parallelepiped and describing it by three inequalities. Use a suitable change of variables such that all the integral limits are constants (as opposed to functions).
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    We can easily write the equations of \(4\) of the faces, two of which are parallel to the \(xy\)-plane and the other two are parallel to the \(yz\)-plane:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z = 0, \quad z = 1, \quad x = 0, \quad \text{and} \quad x = 1. \end{equation*}
    To write the equations of the remaining two slanted faces, we find the normal to the plane, and together with a point on each plane, we compute their equations. Each slanted face contains the direction \(\mathbf{i}\) (an edge parallel to the \(x\)-axis) and the edge direction \(\mathbf{j} + \mathbf{k}\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{n} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} \amp \mathbf{j} \amp \mathbf{k} \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} = -\mathbf{j} + \mathbf{k}. \end{equation*}
    Using the points \(\mathbf{0}\) and \((0,3,0)\) on the two faces,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} D_1 = \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{0} = 0 \amp\;\Rightarrow\; y - z = 0,\\ D_2 = \mathbf{n} \cdot (0,3,0)^T = -3 \amp\;\Rightarrow\; y - z = 3. \end{align*}
    Thus we can describe the parallelepiped as the region between the six faces—in other words, by the three inequalities
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R = \{ (x,y,z) : 0 \le x \le 1,\ 0 \le z \le 1,\ 0 \le y - z \le 3 \}. \end{equation*}
    We can now transform the region \(R\) into a box \(S\) using the transformation \(u = x\text{,}\) \(v = z\text{,}\) and \(w = y - z\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} S = \{ (u,v,w) : 0 \le u \le 1,\ 0 \le v \le 1,\ 0 \le w \le 3 \}. \end{equation*}
    Note that according to the inverse function theorem, the matrix inverse of the Jacobian matrix of an invertible function is the Jacobian matrix of the inverse function, exactly as in (2.9.5). Using (2.9.2) in the easy direction,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(x,y,z)} = \begin{vmatrix} 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp -1 \end{vmatrix} = -1 \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} = (-1)^{-1} = -1. \end{equation*}
    For the volume of the parallelepiped, (2.9.4) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V_P = \iiint_R dx\, dy\, dz = \iiint_S \lvert -1 \rvert\, du\, dv\, dw = \int_0^1 \int_0^1 \int_0^3 dw\, dv\, du = 3. \end{equation*}
    The animation in Figure 2.9.16 shows the shear that straightens the parallelepiped into a box.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.9.15.

    Finally, we would like to mention that there is a much easier way of calculating the volume of a parallelepiped (using the so-called box product); however, the method above is a rich example in calculus and definitely worth learning. For example, you may need to integrate a function over the parallelepiped, which cannot be done using the box product.

    🔗

    Example 2.9.18. Volume of an Ellipsoid.

    Use the method of substitution in multiple integrals to show that the volume of the ellipsoid
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} + \frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1 \end{equation*}
    is equal to \(\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\, abc\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    The ellipsoid is a stretched sphere, so we use the transformation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T: \quad x = au, \qquad y = bv, \qquad z = cw, \end{equation*}
    which carries the unit ball \(S\text{,}\) bounded by \(u^2 + v^2 + w^2 = 1\text{,}\) onto the solid ellipsoid \(E\text{,}\) as illustrated in Figure 2.9.20. By (2.9.2), the Jacobian is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} J(u,v,w) = \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} = \begin{vmatrix} a \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp b \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp c \end{vmatrix} = abc. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, by (2.9.4),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V_E = \iiint_E dx\, dy\, dz = \iiint_S \left| \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} \right| du\, dv\, dw = abc \iiint_S du\, dv\, dw = \frac{4\pi}{3}\, abc, \end{equation*}
    since the last integral is the volume of a sphere of radius one, \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\text{.}\) The animation in Figure 2.9.19 shows the unit sphere stretching by the factors \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(c\) along the three axes, scaling its volume by \(abc\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.9.21. Volume Between a Paraboloid and a Plane.

    Find the volume of the solid enclosed by \(z = 9 - x^2 - 9y^2\) and \(z = 5\text{.}\) Do so by converting the integral \(\displaystyle\iiint dz\, dy\, dx\) to another triple integral such that the limits of two of the variables are constants.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 3.
    The two surfaces meet where \(9 - x^2 - 9y^2 = 5\text{,}\) that is, on the ellipse \(x^2 + 9y^2 = 4\text{.}\) So the projection of the solid onto the \(xy\)-plane is the region inside this ellipse, and above each of its points \(5 \le z \le 9 - x^2 - 9y^2\text{.}\) The ellipse becomes a circle under the transformation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T_1: \quad x = 3u, \qquad y = v, \qquad z = w, \end{equation*}
    whose Jacobian, by (2.9.2), is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} = \begin{vmatrix} 3 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \end{vmatrix} = 3. \end{equation*}
    Under \(T_1\) the boundary \(x^2 + 9y^2 = 4\) of the \(xy\)-projection becomes \(9u^2 + 9v^2 = 4\text{,}\) so the \(uv\)-projection is the disk \(u^2 + v^2 \le \frac{4}{9}\text{,}\) and the bounds \(5 \le z \le 9 - x^2 - 9y^2\) become \(5 \le w \le 9\left(1 - u^2 - v^2\right)\text{.}\) By (2.9.4),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_{xy\text{-proj}} \int_{z=5}^{z=9-x^2-9y^2} dz\, dy\, dx = \iint_{uv\text{-proj}} \int_{w=5}^{w=9(1-u^2-v^2)} 3\, dw\, dv\, du . \end{equation*}
    The \(uv\)-projection is a disk, so a second transformation, to cylindrical coordinates,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T_2: \quad u = r\cos\theta, \qquad v = r\sin\theta, \qquad w = z, \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(r,\theta,z)} = r, \end{equation*}
    turns the disk \(u^2 + v^2 \le \frac{4}{9}\) into \(0 \le r \le \frac{2}{3}\text{,}\) \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\text{,}\) with \(5 \le z \le 9\left(1 - r^2\right)\text{:}\) the limits of \(r\) and \(\theta\) are now constants. Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{align*} V \amp= 3 \int_{r=0}^{r=2/3} \int_{\theta=0}^{\theta=2\pi} \int_{z=5}^{z=9(1-r^2)} r\, dz\, d\theta\, dr = (6\pi) \int_{r=0}^{r=2/3} \left(4r - 9r^3\right) dr\\ \amp= (6\pi) \left(2r^2 - \frac{9}{4} r^4\right)\Bigg|_{r=0}^{r=2/3} = (6\pi)\left(\frac{8}{9} - \frac{4}{9}\right) = (6\pi)\left(\frac{4}{9}\right) = \frac{8\pi}{3}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.9.4 Activity: Watching a Substitution Transform a Region

    Activity 2.9.1. From a Rectangle to a Curved Region.

    Use the transformation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x = \frac{u^2}{4}, \qquad y = \frac{uv}{4} \end{equation*}
    to compute
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_E \frac{x}{y^2}\, dx\, dy, \end{equation*}
    where
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} E = \left\{ (x,y) \in \R^2 : 1 \le x \le 4,\ \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{x} \le y \le \frac{5}{2}\sqrt{x} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    Also, make sure to graph the region of integration in the \(uv\)-plane. Note: choose a suitable substitution such that the limits of integration are numbers as opposed to functions.
    🔗
    The interactive below shows exactly this transformation at work: a rectangle in the \(uv\)-plane (left) is carried by \(T(u,v) = \left( \tfrac{u^2}{4}, \tfrac{uv}{4} \right)\) onto a curved region in the \(xy\)-plane (right). Drag the rectangle’s corner and adjust its dimensions until its image is exactly the region \(E\text{,}\) and use what you see to read off the constant \(uv\)-limits.
    🔗
    

    Instructions.
    Use the “Dimensions” sliders to change its length and width; the shaded region in the \(xy\)-plane is its image under \(x = u^2/4\text{,}\) \(y = uv/4\text{.}\) Press “Animation” to watch the rectangle sweep out its image, and “Clear Traces” to start over.

    🔗
    Solution.
    Reading off the \(uv\)-rectangle. The transformation is already solved for \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) so we can translate the description of \(E\) directly. Since \(x = u^2/4\) with \(u \ge 0\text{,}\) we have \(u = 2\sqrt{x}\text{,}\) and therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 1 \le x \le 4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2 \le u \le 4. \end{equation*}
    For the \(y\)-bounds, note that \(\sqrt{x} = u/2\text{,}\) so the curves \(y = \tfrac12\sqrt{x}\) and \(y = \tfrac52\sqrt{x}\) become \(y = u/4\) and \(y = 5u/4\text{.}\) Since \(y = uv/4\text{,}\) dividing these inequalities by \(u/4 > 0\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \tfrac12\sqrt{x} \le y \le \tfrac52\sqrt{x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1 \le v \le 5. \end{equation*}
    Thus the region \(E\) is the image of the rectangle with constant limits
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} S = \{ (u,v) : 2 \le u \le 4,\ 1 \le v \le 5 \}, \end{equation*}
    which is exactly the rectangle the interactive carries onto \(E\text{.}\) This settles the graph in the \(uv\)-plane: it is the axis-aligned box \([2,4]\times[1,5]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The Jacobian. Because the transformation is given in the direction \(x = x(u,v)\text{,}\) \(y = y(u,v)\text{,}\) we compute \(\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)\) directly:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \begin{vmatrix} x_u \amp x_v \\ y_u \amp y_v \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} u/2 \amp 0 \\ v/4 \amp u/4 \end{vmatrix} = \frac{u}{2}\cdot\frac{u}{4} - 0 = \frac{u^2}{8}. \end{equation*}
    On \(S\) we have \(u \ge 2 > 0\text{,}\) so \(\left| \partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v) \right| = u^2/8\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The integrand. With \(x = u^2/4\) and \(y = uv/4\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{y^2} = \frac{u^2/4}{(uv/4)^2} = \frac{u^2/4}{u^2 v^2/16} = \frac{4}{v^2}. \end{equation*}
    Evaluating. Applying (2.9.3), the integral separates into a product of single-variable integrals:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \iint_E \frac{x}{y^2}\, dx\, dy \amp= \iint_S \frac{4}{v^2}\cdot\frac{u^2}{8}\, du\, dv = \frac12 \int_1^5 \int_2^4 \frac{u^2}{v^2}\, du\, dv\\ \amp= \frac12 \left( \int_2^4 u^2\, du \right) \left( \int_1^5 \frac{dv}{v^2} \right) = \frac12 \cdot \frac{56}{3} \cdot \frac{4}{5} = \frac{112}{15}. \end{align*}
    The awkward \(\sqrt{x}\)-limits in the \(xy\)-plane have become the constant limits of a rectangle, and the integrand collapses to a function of \(v\) alone.
