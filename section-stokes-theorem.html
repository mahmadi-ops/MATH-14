    Section 3.2 Stokes’ Theorem

    The circulation-curl form of Green’s theorem, Theorem 3.1.12, traded a circulation around a closed curve in the plane for a double integral of \((\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{k}\) over the region the curve encloses. Nothing in that statement really needs the region to be flat. If we bend the region up out of the plane into a surface \(S\) in space, keeping \(C\) as its boundary, the same exchange still works: the circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around \(C\) equals the flux of the curl of \(\mathbf{F}\) through \(S\text{.}\) That is Stokes’ theorem, the promise made in the footnote of Subsection 3.1.3.
    🔗
    It completes the family. Section 3.3 turned a flux integral over a closed surface into a triple integral of the divergence; Stokes’ theorem turns a line integral around the boundary of an open surface into a surface integral of the curl. Both say the same kind of thing: what happens on the boundary of a piece is the accumulation, over the piece, of a derivative of the field.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2.1 Curl in Three Dimensions

    In Subsection 3.1.2 we only needed the component of the curl along \(\mathbf{k}\text{,}\) because in the plane a paddle wheel can only spin about an axis perpendicular to the page. In space the axis can point anywhere, so the curl is a vector: it points along the axis the paddle wheel spins about, and its length is the circulation density about that axis.
    🔗

    Definition 3.2.1.

    The curl of the vector field \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j} + P\,\mathbf{k}\text{,}\) whose components have continuous first partial derivatives, is
    🔗
    \begin{align} \operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} = \nabla \times \mathbf{F} \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} \amp \mathbf{j} \amp \mathbf{k} \\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ M \amp N \amp P \end{vmatrix}\tag{3.2.1}\\ \amp = \left( \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial N}{\partial z} \right)\mathbf{i} + \left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} \right)\mathbf{j}\notag\\ \amp \qquad + \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right)\mathbf{k}.\notag \end{align}
    🔗
    The \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of (3.2.1) is exactly the quantity \(\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y}\) of (3.1.3), so the two-dimensional curl of Subsection 3.1.2 was one component of this vector all along.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.2. Computing a Curl.

    Find \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\) for \(\mathbf{F} = xz\,\mathbf{i} + xyz\,\mathbf{j} - y^2\,\mathbf{k}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Here \(M = xz\text{,}\) \(N = xyz\text{,}\) and \(P = -y^2\text{.}\) Taking the three components of (3.2.1) in turn,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial N}{\partial z} \amp = -2y - xy,\\ \frac{\partial M}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} \amp = x - 0 = x,\\ \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \amp = yz - 0 = yz, \end{align*}
    so that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = -(2y + xy)\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j} + yz\,\mathbf{k}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Two facts about the curl are worth recording, both of which we have already met in disguise. First, a field is conservative exactly when its curl vanishes: if \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla f\) then \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\text{,}\) which is the component test (3.1.7) for the conservative fields of Section 1.5. Second, \(\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0\text{:}\) a curl field has no divergence, so by Theorem 3.3.5 its flux through any closed surface is zero. We will use that second fact at the end of this section.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2.2 Stokes’ Theorem

    Stokes’ theorem needs the orientation of the surface and the direction of travel along its boundary to agree. The rule is the right-hand rule: if the thumb of your right hand points along the unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\text{,}\) your fingers curl in the positive direction along the boundary curve \(C\text{.}\) Equivalently, walking along \(C\) with your head pointing in the direction of \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\text{,}\) the surface is on your left. This is the orientation drawn in Figure 3.2.6; reversing \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) reverses the direction on \(C\) and changes the sign of both sides of the theorem.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.2.4. Stokes’ Theorem.

    Let \(S\) be a piecewise smooth oriented surface with unit normal field \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\text{,}\) whose boundary is a piecewise smooth simple closed curve \(C\) traversed in the direction given by the right-hand rule. Let \(\mathbf{F}\) be a vector field whose components have continuous first partial derivatives on an open region containing \(S\text{.}\) Then the circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around \(C\) equals the flux of \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\) through \(S\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \underbrace{\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}}_{\text{circulation around the boundary } C} = \underbrace{\iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma}_{\text{flux of the curl through } S}.\tag{3.2.2} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Remark 3.2.5.

    Green’s theorem is the flat case. If \(S\) is a region \(R\) of the \(xy\)-plane with \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{k}\text{,}\) then \(d\sigma = dx\,dy\) and \((\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{k}\) is the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of the curl, so (3.2.2) becomes (3.1.5) word for word.
    🔗
    🔗
    Why is the theorem true? The animation in Figure 3.2.7 tells the story without any algebra. Chop \(S\) into tiny, nearly flat patches; on each one, \((\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is the circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) per unit area, so the patch contributes \((\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\,\Delta\sigma\) of circulation. Add up the patches: every interior edge is shared by two of them and travelled in opposite directions, so the interior cancels completely and only the boundary \(C\) survives. Shrinking the patches turns the sum into the flux integral, and (3.2.2) falls out.
    🔗
    The theorem is most useful when one of the two sides is much easier than the other. Reading it from left to right replaces an awkward line integral by a flux integral, as in Example 3.2.10; reading it from right to left replaces an awkward surface integral by a line integral around the boundary, as in Example 3.2.20.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.8. Both Sides of Stokes’ Theorem.

    Verify (3.2.2) for the field \(\mathbf{F} = -y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\) and the upper hemisphere \(S\) of \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\text{,}\) oriented by its upward unit normal.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The boundary of \(S\) is the unit circle \(C\) in the \(xy\)-plane. The right-hand rule with an upward normal makes \(C\) counterclockwise seen from above, so we parametrize it by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \cos t\,\mathbf{i} + \sin t\,\mathbf{j}\text{,}\) \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\text{.}\) Along \(C\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t)\bigr) \amp = \left\langle -\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 0 \right\rangle ,\\ \mathbf{r}'(t) \amp = \left\langle -\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 0 \right\rangle ,\\ \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{r}' \amp = \sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1 , \end{align*}
    so the circulation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} 1\, dt = 2\pi . \end{equation*}
    For the other side, (3.2.1) gives \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = 2\,\mathbf{k}\text{,}\) a constant field. On the hemisphere the outward unit normal is \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = x\,\mathbf{i} + y\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k}\) by (2.10.4), so \((\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = 2z\) and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \iint_S 2z\, d\sigma . \end{equation*}
    Parametrizing the hemisphere by (2.10.1) with \(d\sigma = \sin\phi\, d\phi\, d\theta\) from (2.10.3) and \(z = \cos\phi\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \iint_S 2z\, d\sigma \amp = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2} 2\cos\phi \sin\phi\, d\phi\, d\theta\\ \amp = \int_0^{2\pi} \Bigl[\sin^2\phi\Bigr]_0^{\pi/2} d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} 1\, d\theta = 2\pi , \end{align*}
    which matches the circulation, as Theorem 3.2.4 promises.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.2.10. A Circulation That Is Easier as a Flux.

    Evaluate \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for \(\mathbf{F} = -y^2\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j} + z^2\,\mathbf{k}\text{,}\) where \(C\) is the curve in which the plane \(y + z = 2\) cuts the cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise when seen from above. The curve is drawn in Figure 3.2.11.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    Parametrizing \(C\) and integrating directly is possible but unpleasant. Instead take \(S\) to be the flat elliptical region that \(C\) bounds in the plane \(y + z = 2\text{,}\) that is the graph of \(z = 2 - y\) over the unit disk \(R\) in the \(xy\)-plane. For a graph \(z = g(x,y)\) with an upward normal, (2.10.6) with \(\mathbf{r}(x,y)\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \left\langle -g_x,\ -g_y,\ 1 \right\rangle dx\, dy = \left\langle 0,\ 1,\ 1 \right\rangle dx\, dy , \end{equation*}
    and an upward normal is the one the right-hand rule pairs with a counterclockwise \(C\text{.}\) From (3.2.1),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left\langle 0,\ 0,\ 1 + 2y \right\rangle , \end{equation*}
    so \((\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \left\langle 0, 1, 1 \right\rangle = 1 + 2y\) and (3.2.2) turns the line integral into a double integral over the unit disk:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \amp = \iint_R \left(1 + 2y\right) dx\, dy\\ \amp = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{1} \left(1 + 2r\sin\theta\right) r\, dr\, d\theta = \pi . \end{align*}
    The term \(2y\) integrates to zero because the disk is symmetric about \(y = 0\text{,}\) and what is left is the area \(\pi\) of the disk.
    🔗
    🔗
    The next example shows the theorem at its most merciless: one term of the field cannot be integrated in closed form at all, and Stokes’ theorem simply erases it.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.12. A Term That Cannot Be Integrated.

    Evaluate \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} = \left[\cos\left(x^{100}\right) + 3y\right]\mathbf{i} + 4z\,\mathbf{j} + 2y\,\mathbf{k}, \end{equation*}
    where \(C\) is the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\text{,}\) \(z = 2\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise when seen from above.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 3.
    The term \(\cos\left(x^{100}\right)\) makes a direct evaluation hopeless—its antiderivative has no closed form. But Stokes’ theorem only ever sees the field through its curl, and by (3.2.1),
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} \amp = \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2y) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(4z) \right) \mathbf{i} + 0\,\mathbf{j}\\ \amp \quad + \left( 0 - \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left[\cos\left(x^{100}\right) + 3y\right] \right) \mathbf{k} = -2\,\mathbf{i} - 3\,\mathbf{k}: \end{align*}
    the monster differentiates to zero. For the surface we take the cheapest thing with boundary \(C\text{:}\) the flat disk \(S = \left\{ (x,y,z) : x^2 + y^2 \le 9,\ z = 2 \right\}\text{,}\) whose unit normal (paired with the counterclockwise orientation of \(C\) by the right-hand rule) is \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{k}\text{.}\) Then \((\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = -3\) is constant, so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = -3 \cdot \text{Area}(S) = -3 \left(9\pi\right) = -27\pi . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The same trick works when the curve is tilted—the flat surface it bounds just tilts with it.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.14. A Tilted Circle.

    Evaluate \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} = \ln\left(1 + x^8\right)\mathbf{i} - z\,\mathbf{j} + y\,\mathbf{k}, \end{equation*}
    where \(C\) is the intersection of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9\) with the plane \(z = x\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise when seen from above.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 4.
    The plane \(z = x\) passes through the centre of the sphere, so \(C\) is a great circle: centre \((0,0,0)\) and radius \(3\text{.}\) The \(\ln\left(1 + x^8\right)\) again rules out a direct attack, and again it dies under the curl: by (3.2.1),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y) - \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(-z) \right) \mathbf{i} + 0\,\mathbf{j} + 0\,\mathbf{k} = 2\,\mathbf{i} . \end{equation*}
    Take \(S\) to be the flat disk that \(C\) bounds in the plane \(z = x\text{.}\) The gradient of \(z - x\) is \(-\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{k}\text{,}\) so the upward unit normal of \(S\) (the one the right-hand rule pairs with counterclockwise-from-above) is \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{k})\text{,}\) and the integrand
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = 2\,\mathbf{i} \cdot \frac{-\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{k}}{\sqrt{2}} = -\sqrt{2} \end{equation*}
    is constant. Since \(S\) is a disk of radius \(3\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\sqrt{2}\ \text{Area}(S) = -\sqrt{2}\left(9\pi\right) = -9\sqrt{2}\,\pi . \end{equation*}
    If the constant-integrand shortcut goes unnoticed, the standard graph protocol still works: on \(S\text{,}\) \(z = f(x,y) = x\text{,}\) so \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \left\langle -f_x, -f_y, 1 \right\rangle dx\,dy = \left\langle -1, 0, 1 \right\rangle dx\,dy\) and \((\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = -2\, dx\,dy\text{.}\) Substituting \(z = x\) into the sphere, the shadow of \(S\) in the \(xy\)-plane is the ellipse \(2x^2 + y^2 \le 9\text{,}\) with semiaxes \(3/\sqrt{2}\) and \(3\) and area \(9\pi/\sqrt{2}\text{.}\) Then \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -2 \left( 9\pi/\sqrt{2} \right) = -9\sqrt{2}\,\pi\text{,}\) as before.
    🔗
    🔗
    When the boundary is made of straight segments, both sides of (3.2.2) are computable, and the comparison is instructive: the direct route costs three parametrizations, the Stokes route one double integral.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.16. Around a Triangle, Twice.

    Evaluate \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} = (y - z)\,\mathbf{i} + (x + z)\,\mathbf{j} + \left(x^2 + y\right)\mathbf{k}, \end{equation*}
    where \(C\) goes from \((2,0,0)\) to \((0,2,0)\) to \((0,0,4)\) and back to \((2,0,0)\) along straight line segments, as in Figure 3.2.17. Do the computation twice: directly, and by Stokes’ theorem.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 5.
    Directly. The segment \(C_1\) from \((2,0,0)\) to \((0,2,0)\) is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = (2-2t,\ 2t,\ 0)\text{,}\) \(0 \le t \le 1\text{,}\) with \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = (-2,\ 2,\ 0)\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \int_{C_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \amp = \int_0^1 \left(2t,\ 2-2t,\ (2-2t)^2 + 2t\right) \cdot (-2,\ 2,\ 0)\, dt\\ \amp = \int_0^1 (4 - 8t)\, dt = 0 . \end{align*}
    The segment \(C_2\) from \((0,2,0)\) to \((0,0,4)\) is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = (0,\ 2-2t,\ 4t)\) with \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = (0,\ -2,\ 4)\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \int_{C_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \amp = \int_0^1 \left(2 - 6t,\ 4t,\ 2 - 2t\right) \cdot (0,\ -2,\ 4)\, dt\\ \amp = \int_0^1 (8 - 16t)\, dt = 0 , \end{align*}
    and the segment \(C_3\) from \((0,0,4)\) back to \((2,0,0)\) is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = (2t,\ 0,\ 4-4t)\) with \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = (2,\ 0,\ -4)\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \int_{C_3} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \amp = \int_0^1 \left(4t - 4,\ 4 - 2t,\ 4t^2\right) \cdot (2,\ 0,\ -4)\, dt\\ \amp = \int_0^1 \left(8t - 8 - 16t^2\right) dt = -\frac{28}{3} . \end{align*}
    The total is \(0 + 0 - \frac{28}{3} = -\frac{28}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By Stokes’ theorem. By (3.2.1), \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = (1-1)\,\mathbf{i} + (-1 - 2x)\,\mathbf{j} + (1-1)\,\mathbf{k} = -(1 + 2x)\,\mathbf{j}\text{.}\) The triangle lies in the plane \(\frac{x}{2} + \frac{y}{2} + \frac{z}{4} = 1\text{,}\) that is, the graph of \(z = 4 - 2x - 2y\) over the shadow triangle \(R = \left\{ (x,y) : x \ge 0,\ y \ge 0,\ x + y \le 2 \right\}\text{.}\) The traversal \((2,0,0) \to (0,2,0) \to (0,0,4)\) is counterclockwise seen from above, which pairs with the upward normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \left\langle 2,\ 2,\ 1 \right\rangle dx\,dy\text{.}\) So
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \amp = \iint_R \left\langle 0,\ -(1+2x),\ 0 \right\rangle \cdot \left\langle 2,\ 2,\ 1 \right\rangle dx\,dy\\ \amp = \iint_R (-2 - 4x)\, dx\,dy\\ \amp = -2\,\text{Area}(R) - 4 \iint_R x\, dx\,dy = -2(2) - 4\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) = -\frac{28}{3} , \end{align*}
    using \(\iint_R x\, dx\,dy = \int_0^2 x(2-x)\, dx = \frac{4}{3}\text{.}\) The two answers agree, and the second computation is the one to remember.
    🔗
    🔗
    Finally, before reaching for Stokes’ theorem at all, it pays to glance at the curl: if it vanishes, no surface is needed.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.18. The Curve Nobody Can Parametrize.

    Evaluate \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} = \left(e^x + y + z\right)\mathbf{i} + (x + z)\,\mathbf{j} + (x + y)\,\mathbf{k}, \end{equation*}
    where \(C\) is the intersection of the ellipsoid \(x^2 + \frac{y^2}{4} + \frac{z^2}{9} = 1\) with the paraboloid \(z = 2x^2 + y^2\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise when seen from above.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 6.
    The curve is a warped loop around the paraboloid—solving the two equations simultaneously for a parametrization would be miserable, and whatever surface it bounds is worse. But by (3.2.1),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = (1 - 1)\,\mathbf{i} + (1 - 1)\,\mathbf{j} + (1 - 1)\,\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{0} . \end{equation*}
    By the component test (3.1.7), this \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative—indeed \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla\!\left(e^x + xy + xz + yz\right)\)—so whichever surface \(S\) the loop bounds,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \iint_S \mathbf{0} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = 0 , \end{equation*}
    and the geometry of \(C\) never enters. Closed curve, conservative field, zero work—exactly as in Section 1.5.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.2.3 Only the Boundary Matters

    The right-hand side of (3.2.2) mentions \(S\text{,}\) but the left-hand side does not. So if two oriented surfaces \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) share the same boundary curve \(C\text{,}\) with orientations that give the same direction on \(C\text{,}\) both flux integrals equal the same circulation:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \iint_{S_1} (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_{S_2} (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma .\tag{3.2.3} \end{equation}
    This is the surface analogue of the path independence of Section 1.5, and it has the same practical use: when a surface is unpleasant, trade it for any other surface with the same boundary. It also explains the identity \(\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0\) noted earlier. Gluing \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) along \(C\) makes a closed surface; the two flux integrals appear with opposite signs once both normals point outward, so the total flux of \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\) through any closed surface is zero.
    🔗

    Example 3.2.20. Trading a Paraboloid for a Disk.

    Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma\) for \(\mathbf{F} = y\,\mathbf{i} + z\,\mathbf{j} + x\,\mathbf{k}\text{,}\) where \(S\) is the part of the paraboloid \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\) above the \(xy\)-plane, oriented upward.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The boundary of \(S\) is the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\) in the plane \(z = 0\text{,}\) and the upward orientation makes it counterclockwise seen from above. By (3.2.3) we may replace the paraboloid by the flat disk \(S_2\) of radius \(2\) in the \(xy\)-plane, oriented upward, which has the same boundary. On that disk \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbf{k}\) and \(d\sigma = dx\,dy\text{.}\)
    🔗
    From (3.2.1), \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = -\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j} - \mathbf{k}\text{,}\) so \((\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{k} = -1\) and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \iint_{S_2} (-1)\, dx\, dy = -\left(\text{area of } S_2\right) = -4\pi . \end{equation*}
    As a check, the circulation around the boundary gives the same number. With \(\mathbf{r}(t) = 2\cos t\,\mathbf{i} + 2\sin t\,\mathbf{j}\) and \(z = 0\) we have \(\mathbf{F} = \left\langle 2\sin t,\ 0,\ 2\cos t \right\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = \left\langle -2\sin t,\ 2\cos t,\ 0 \right\rangle\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^{2\pi} \left(-4\sin^2 t\right) dt = -4\pi . \end{equation*}
