    Section 3 Line Integrals

    In this section we study line integrals of scalar functions. We develop the idea through a concrete problem: finding the mass of a curved wire when we know its density—its mass per unit length—at every point. The strategy is the one behind every integral: chop the wire into tiny pieces, multiply the density of each piece by its length, and add up the results.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.1 Line Integrals of Scalar Functions

    Suppose we would like to compute the mass of a wire that does not have a trivial shape (such as a straight line). If the wire were straight and its density constant, the mass would simply be density times length. For a curved wire whose density \(\rho(x,y,z)\) varies from point to point, neither shortcut is available—but we can recover the same simple rule on small pieces.
    🔗
    Assume the wire is represented by a curve \(C\) in space, parametrized by \(t\in[a,b]\text{,}\) as in Figure 3.1. Divide the wire into \(n\) small portions, and denote the length of the \(k\)th portion by \(\Delta s_k\text{.}\) Pick a sample point \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\) on this portion. If the portion is short, the density is nearly constant along it, so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{mass of the } k\text{th portion} \approx \rho\left(x_k,y_k,z_k\right)\,\Delta s_k, \end{equation*}
    and summing over all portions,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{mass of the wire} \approx \sum_{k=1}^{n} \rho\left(x_k,y_k,z_k\right)\,\Delta s_k. \end{equation*}
    This is only an approximation, because the density is not actually constant over each portion. How can we make the calculation exact? We take the limit in which every portion becomes infinitely small, that is, \(\Delta s_k\to 0\text{,}\) or equivalently \(n\to\infty\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{mass of the wire} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n} \rho\left(x_k,y_k,z_k\right)\,\Delta s_k. \end{equation*}
    This limit is denoted by the integral
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{mass of the wire} = \int_C \rho\left(x,y,z\right)\,ds, \end{equation*}
    read “the integral of \(\rho\) over the curve \(C\text{.}\)” The process is animated in Figure 3.2: as the partition refines, the sums settle down to the value of the integral.
    🔗
    Our final task is to write the line element \(ds\) in terms of \(dt\text{,}\) so the integral becomes an ordinary integral we can evaluate. Exactly as in Section 2, the line element is the length of an infinitesimal displacement in \(\R^3\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} ds=\sqrt{(dx)^2+(dy)^2+(dz)^2}, \end{equation*}
    and factoring out \(dt\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \text{mass of the wire} \amp = \int_C \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right)\,\frac{ds}{dt}\,dt\\ \amp = \int_C \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt. \end{align*}
    The square root is exactly the length of the velocity vector
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}(t)=\mathbf{r}'(t) =\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)\mathbf{i} +\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)\mathbf{j} +\left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)\mathbf{k} =\left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\frac{dy}{dt},\frac{dz}{dt}\right\rangle, \end{equation*}
    so the formula takes the tidy form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{mass of the wire} = \int_a^b \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\right\rVert\,dt. \end{equation*}
    There is nothing special about density here. In general, a scalar line integral has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_C f\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right)\,ds = \int_a^b f\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\right\rVert\,dt, \end{equation*}
    where \(f\) is a scalar-valued function and the curve \(C\) specifies the region of integration. Once the curve is parametrized in terms of \(t\text{,}\) the region of integration translates to \(a\le t\le b\text{.}\) In words: to compute a line integral, trade the geometric element \(ds\) for the parameter element \(\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\,dt\). Two sanity checks connect this to what we already know. Taking \(f=1\) recovers the arc length \(\int_C ds=\int_a^b \lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\,dt\) of Section 2—total length is the “mass” of a wire of density one. And \(\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\) is the speed \(\left|\mathbf{v}(t)\right|\) from that section, so the factor converts time spent into distance covered.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.3. Mass and length of a helical wire.

    Consider a wire of length \(l\) and mass \(m\text{.}\) The wire is helix-shaped, with its bottom end located at \((1,0,0)\) and parametric equation
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t)=\langle \cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle,\qquad t\ge 0. \end{equation*}
    Its density (mass per unit length) is \(\rho(x,y,z)=4x^2y^2\) kg/m. Assuming that \(m=\dfrac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}\) kg, calculate the length \(l\) of the wire.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The wire occupies the part of the helix with \(0\le t\le b\text{,}\) where the upper limit \(b\) is unknown: it marks where the wire ends. Our plan has two steps. First we use the known mass in the formula
    \begin{equation} m = \int_0^{b} \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\right\rVert\,dt\tag{3.1} \end{equation}
    to find \(b\text{;}\) then the length is the arc length \(l=\int_0^b \lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\rVert\,dt\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First we find \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) and its length:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{r}'(t) \amp = \langle -\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 1\rangle,\\ \left\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\right\rVert \amp = \sqrt{\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t+1}=\sqrt{2}. \end{align*}
    Next we write the density along the curve in terms of \(t\text{,}\) using the double-angle identity \(2\sin t\cos t=\sin(2t)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) = 4x^2y^2 = 4\cos^2 t\,\sin^2 t = \left(2\sin t\cos t\right)^2 = \sin^2(2t). \end{equation*}
    Substituting into (3.1) and using the half-angle identity \(\sin^2(2t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1-\cos(4t)\bigr)\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} m \amp = \sqrt{2}\int_0^{b}\sin^2(2t)\,dt = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_0^{b}\bigl(1-\cos(4t)\bigr)\,dt\\ \amp = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(t-\frac{1}{4}\sin(4t)\right)\bigg|_0^{b} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(b-\frac{1}{4}\sin(4b)\right). \end{align*}
    Setting this equal to the given mass \(m=\dfrac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}\) and multiplying both sides by \(\sqrt{2}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} b-\frac{1}{4}\sin(4b)=\frac{\pi}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b=\frac{\pi}{2}, \end{equation*}
    since \(\sin(4\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2})=\sin(2\pi)=0\text{.}\) (This solution is the only one: the left-hand side has derivative \(1-\cos(4b)\ge 0\text{,}\) so it is increasing and crosses the value \(\pi/2\) exactly once.)
    🔗
    The length of the wire is then the arc length from \(t=0\) to \(t=\pi/2\text{.}\) Because the speed is the constant \(\sqrt{2}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} l=\int_0^{\pi/2}\left\lVert\mathbf{r}'(t)\right\rVert\,dt =\sqrt{2}\int_0^{\pi/2}dt =\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\approx 2.22 \text{ meters}. \end{equation*}
    The wire—a quarter turn of the helix from \((1,0,0)\) up to \((0,1,\pi/2)\)—is shown in Figure 3.4, and the accumulation of its mass is animated in Figure 3.5.
