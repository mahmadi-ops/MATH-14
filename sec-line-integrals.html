    Section 1.3 Line Integrals

    In this section we study line integrals of scalar functions. We develop the idea through a concrete problem: finding the mass of a curved wire when we know its density—its mass per unit length—at every point. The strategy is the one behind every integral: chop the wire into tiny pieces, multiply the density of each piece by its length, and add up the results.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.1 Line Integrals of Scalar Functions

    Suppose we would like to compute the mass of a wire that does not have a trivial shape (such as a straight line). If the wire were straight and its density constant, the mass would simply be density times length. For a curved wire whose density \(\rho(x,y,z)\) varies from point to point, neither shortcut is available—but we can approximate mass of small portions of the wire by treating each as if it were straight and had constant density.
    🔗
    Assume the wire is represented by a curve \(C\) in space, parametrized by \(t\in[a,b]\text{,}\) as in Figure 1.3.1. Divide the wire into \(n\) small portions, and denote the length of the \(k\)th portion by \(\Delta s_k\text{.}\) Pick a sample point \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\) on this portion. If the portion is short, the density is nearly constant along it, so
    \begin{gather} \text{mass of the } k\text{th portion} \approx \rho\left(x_k,y_k,z_k\right)\,\Delta s_k,\tag{1.3.1} \end{gather}
    🔗
    and summing (1.3.1) over all portions,
    \begin{gather} \text{mass of the wire} \approx \sum_{k=1}^{n} \rho\left(x_k,y_k,z_k\right)\,\Delta s_k.\tag{1.3.2} \end{gather}
    🔗
    The estimate (1.3.2) is only an approximation, because the density is not actually constant over each portion. How can we make the calculation exact? We take the limit in which every portion becomes infinitely small, that is, \(\Delta s_k\to 0\text{,}\) or equivalently \(n\to\infty\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather} \text{mass of the wire} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n} \rho\left(x_k,y_k,z_k\right)\,\Delta s_k.\tag{1.3.3} \end{gather}
    🔗
    The limit in (1.3.3) is denoted by the integral
    \begin{gather} \text{mass of the wire} = \int_C \rho\left(x,y,z\right)\,ds,\tag{1.3.4} \end{gather}
    🔗
    which is read as “the line integral of \(\rho\) over the curve \(C\text{.}\)” The process is animated in Figure 1.3.2: as the partition refines, the sums settle down to the value of the integral.
    🔗
    Our final task is to write the line element \(ds\) in terms of \(dt\text{,}\) so the integral becomes an ordinary integral we can evaluate. Exactly as in (1.2.3) of Section 1.2, the line element is the length of an infinitesimal displacement in \(\R^3\text{,}\)
    \begin{gather} ds=\sqrt{(dx)^2+(dy)^2+(dz)^2},\tag{1.3.5} \end{gather}
    🔗
    so substituting (1.3.5) into (1.3.4) and factoring out \(dt\) gives
    \begin{align} \text{mass of the wire} \amp = \int_C \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right)\,\frac{ds}{dt}\,dt\notag\\ \amp = \int_C \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right)\notag\\ \amp \qquad\qquad \times \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt.\tag{1.3.6} \end{align}
    🔗
    The square root in (1.3.6) is exactly the length of the velocity vector
    \begin{align} \mathbf{v}(t) \amp = \frac{d\mathbf{r}(t)}{dt}\notag\\ \amp = \left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)\mathbf{i} + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)\mathbf{j} + \left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)\mathbf{k}\notag\\ \amp = \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt},\ \frac{dz}{dt}\right\rangle,\tag{1.3.7} \end{align}
    🔗
    so (1.3.7) turns (1.3.6) into the tidy form
    \begin{gather} \text{mass of the wire} = \int_a^b \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt.\tag{1.3.8} \end{gather}
    🔗
    There is nothing special about density here. In general, a scalar line integral has the form
    \begin{gather} \int_C f\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right)\,ds = \int_a^b f\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt,\tag{1.3.9} \end{gather}
    🔗
    where \(f\) is a scalar-valued function and the curve \(C\) specifies the region of integration. Once the curve is parametrized in terms of \(t\text{,}\) the region of integration translates to \(a\le t\le b\text{.}\) In words: to compute a line integral, trade the geometric element \(ds\) for the parameter element \(\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\rVert\,dt\). Two sanity checks connect this to what we already know. Taking \(f=1\) in (1.3.9) recovers the arc length \(\int_C ds=\int_a^b \lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\rVert\,dt\) of (1.2.2)—total length is the “mass” of a wire of density one. And \(\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\rVert\) is exactly the speed from that section, so the factor converts time spent into distance covered.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.3. Mass and length of a helical wire.

    Consider a wire of length \(l\) and mass \(m\text{.}\) The wire is helix-shaped, with its bottom end located at \((1,0,0)\) and parametric equation
    \begin{gather} \mathbf{r}(t)=\langle \cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle,\qquad t\ge 0.\tag{1.3.10} \end{gather}
    Its density (mass per unit length) is \(\rho(x,y,z)=4x^2y^2\) kg/m. Assuming that \(m=\dfrac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}\) kg, calculate the length \(l\) of the wire.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The wire occupies the part of the helix with \(0\le t\le b\text{,}\) where the upper limit \(b\) is unknown: it marks where the wire ends. Our plan has two steps. First we use the known mass in (1.3.8), which for this wire reads
    \begin{gather} m = \int_0^{b} \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt\tag{1.3.11} \end{gather}
    to find \(b\text{;}\) then the length is the arc length \(l=\int_0^b \lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\rVert\,dt\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Differentiating (1.3.10) gives \(\mathbf{v}(t)\) and its length:
    \begin{align} \mathbf{v}(t) \amp = \langle -\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 1\rangle,\notag\\ \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert \amp = \sqrt{\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t+1}=\sqrt{2}.\tag{1.3.12} \end{align}
    🔗
    Next we write the density along the curve in terms of \(t\text{,}\) using the double-angle identity \(2\sin t\cos t=\sin(2t)\text{:}\)
    \begin{align} \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \amp = 4x^2y^2 = 4\cos^2 t\,\sin^2 t\notag\\ \amp = \left(2\sin t\cos t\right)^2 = \sin^2(2t).\tag{1.3.13} \end{align}
    🔗
    Substituting (1.3.12) and (1.3.13) into (1.3.11) and using the half-angle identity \(\sin^2(2t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1-\cos(4t)\bigr)\text{,}\)
    \begin{align} m \amp = \sqrt{2}\int_0^{b}\sin^2(2t)\,dt = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_0^{b}\bigl(1-\cos(4t)\bigr)\,dt\notag\\ \amp = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(t-\frac{1}{4}\sin(4t)\right)\bigg|_0^{b}\notag\\ \amp = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left(b-\frac{1}{4}\sin(4b)\right).\tag{1.3.14} \end{align}
    🔗
    Setting (1.3.14) equal to the given mass \(m=\dfrac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}\) and multiplying both sides by \(\sqrt{2}\text{,}\)
    \begin{gather} b-\frac{1}{4}\sin(4b)=\frac{\pi}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b=\frac{\pi}{2},\tag{1.3.15} \end{gather}
    🔗
    since \(\sin(4\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2})=\sin(2\pi)=0\text{.}\) (This solution is the only one: the left-hand side has derivative \(1-\cos(4b)\ge 0\text{,}\) so it is increasing and crosses the value \(\pi/2\) exactly once.)
    🔗
    With \(b=\pi/2\) from (1.3.15), the length of the wire is the arc length from \(t=0\) to \(t=\pi/2\text{.}\) Because the speed in (1.3.12) is the constant \(\sqrt{2}\text{,}\)
    \begin{align} l \amp = \int_0^{\pi/2} \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt = \sqrt{2}\int_0^{\pi/2}dt\notag\\ \amp = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\approx 2.22 \text{ meters}.\tag{1.3.16} \end{align}
    🔗
    The wire—a quarter turn of the helix from \((1,0,0)\) up to \((0,1,\pi/2)\)—is shown in Figure 1.3.4, and the accumulation of its mass is animated in Figure 1.3.5.

    Example 1.3.6. A wire whose density is read off the picture.

    A wire of length \(40\) meters is parametrized as
    \begin{gather} \mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle t\cos(t),\ t\sin(t),\ \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}\,t^{3/2} \right\rangle ,\tag{1.3.17} \end{gather}
    🔗
    where \(x(t)\text{,}\) \(y(t)\) and \(z(t)\) are in meters and \(t\) is in seconds. Assume that the mass density at a point \(P\) of the wire is
    \begin{equation*} \rho = \frac{1}{d+1} \quad \text{kg/m} , \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where \(d\) is the distance from \(P\) to the \(z\)-axis.
    🔗
    The wire is graphed in Figure 1.3.7. Note that there is a piece of information that has to be extracted from the graph. Calculate the mass of the wire.
    🔗
    Solution.
    What the graph supplies. Equation (1.3.17) is a formula for every \(t\text{,}\) but a wire is a finite piece of that curve, and the formula alone does not say which piece. Figure 1.3.7 does: the wire starts at the origin, and \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{0}\) exactly when \(t=0\text{.}\) So the wire is the piece \(0 \le t \le b\text{,}\) and the unknown upper limit \(b\) is what the given length will pin down.
    🔗
    Speed. Differentiating (1.3.17), the first two components by the product rule and the third by the power rule,
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{v}(t) \amp = \left\langle \cos t - t\sin t,\ \sin t + t\cos t,\ \sqrt{2}\,t^{1/2} \right\rangle ,\\ \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert \amp = \sqrt{\left(\cos t - t\sin t\right)^2 + \left(\sin t + t\cos t\right)^2 + 2t}\\ \amp = \sqrt{1 + t^2 + 2t} = \sqrt{(t+1)^2} = t+1 , \end{align*}
    🔗
    where the cross terms \(\pm 2t\sin t\cos t\) cancelled and \(t+1 \gt 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The upper limit. The length of the wire is the arc length from \(t=0\) to \(t=b\text{,}\) and we are told it is \(40\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 40 = \int_0^b \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert \, dt = \int_0^b (t+1)\,dt = \frac{b^2}{2} + b . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    So \(b^2 + 2b - 80 = (b+10)(b-8) = 0\text{,}\) and since \(b \gt 0\) the wire ends at \(b = 8\) seconds.
    🔗
    The density along the wire. The distance from a point to the \(z\)-axis is \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\text{,}\) and along this curve
    \begin{align*} d \amp = \sqrt{x(t)^2 + y(t)^2} = \sqrt{t^2\cos^2 t + t^2\sin^2 t} = t ,\\ \text{so} \quad \rho \amp = \frac{1}{t+1} . \end{align*}
    🔗
    The mass. Now everything collapses: the density is exactly the reciprocal of the speed, so the two factors in (1.3.8) cancel,
    \begin{align*} M \amp = \int_0^{8} \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt\\ \amp = \int_0^{8} \frac{1}{t+1}\,(t+1)\,dt = \int_0^{8} dt = 8 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    The wire has mass \(8\) kilograms. The cancellation is worth a second look: \(\rho\,ds = \rho \left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert dt = dt\) here, so this wire carries exactly one kilogram for every second of parameter—it is heavy and slow near the origin, light and fast far from the \(z\)-axis, and the two effects balance perfectly.

    Subsection 1.3.2 Concept Check

    This last part of the section is not about computing anything new. It is about reviewing the concepts we have just built. Everything below refers to the two applications developed so far using line integrals, in particular the length \(L\) of a wire and its mass \(M\text{,}\)
    \begin{align} L \amp = \int_C ds = \int_{t=a}^{t=b}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt ,\tag{1.3.18}\\ M \amp = \int_C \rho\left(x,y,z\right) ds\notag\\ \amp = \int_{t=a}^{t=b} \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt ,\tag{1.3.19} \end{align}
    🔗
    where (1.3.19) is (1.3.8) again and (1.3.18) is the case \(\rho = 1\)—the arc length (1.2.2) once more. Assume throughout that the thickness of the wire can be ignored.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.3.8. Reading the two formulas.

    In (1.3.18) and (1.3.19), what do \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) \(t\text{,}\) \(C\text{,}\) \(dt\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\rho(x,y,z)\) represent?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(t\) is the parameter along the curve, which we usually read as time, and \(a\) and \(b\) are its initial and final values, so the wire is traced once as \(t\) runs from \(a\) to \(b\text{.}\) The curve \(C\) is the wire itself: it is the region of integration, and once the wire is parametrized that region becomes the interval \(a \le t \le b\text{.}\) The symbol \(dt\) is the element of that parameter interval. The vector \(\mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{r}'(t)\) is the velocity of the point tracing the wire, so \(\left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\) is its speed and the factor that converts time spent into distance covered. Finally \(\rho(x,y,z)\) is the linear mass density, the mass per unit length at the point \((x,y,z)\) of the wire.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.3.9. The two line elements.

    Take two nearby points of the wire, the ones reached at the parameter values \(t_{k-1}\) and \(t_k\text{.}\) Write \(\Delta \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}(t_k) - \mathbf{r}(t_{k-1})\) for the displacement from the first to the second, and \(\Delta s_k\) for the length of the piece of wire joining them. Intuitively speaking, what do \(ds\) and \(d\mathbf{r}\) represent, and what happens to \(\Delta s_k\) and \(\Delta \mathbf{r}\) in the limit that produces them?
    🔗
    Answer.
    Both describe an infinitesimal step along the wire, but one is a number and the other is a vector. \(d\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{v}(t)\,dt\) is the displacement of that step: it records how far the point moves and in which direction. Its length is the scalar \(ds = \left\lVert d\mathbf{r} \right\rVert = \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt\text{,}\) the arc length of the step—how much wire the step uses up, with no direction attached. Scalar line integrals are built from \(ds\text{;}\) the vector element \(d\mathbf{r}\) is what we will need later, for work integrals.
    🔗
    The limit is the passage from Figure 1.3.10 to those two symbols. Write \(\Delta t_k = t_k - t_{k-1}\text{.}\) Dividing the displacement by \(\Delta t_k\) gives a difference quotient, and letting the two points come together turns it into the derivative:
    \begin{align*} \frac{\Delta \mathbf{r}}{\Delta t_k} \amp = \frac{\mathbf{r}(t_k)-\mathbf{r}(t_{k-1})} {\Delta t_k}\\ \amp \ \xrightarrow[\ \Delta t_k \to 0\ ]{}\ \mathbf{r}'(t) = \mathbf{v}(t) ,\\ \text{so} \quad \Delta \mathbf{r} \amp \ \longrightarrow\ d\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{v}(t)\,dt . \end{align*}
    🔗
    The arc does the same thing one step behind. In Figure 1.3.10 the chord cuts across the bend, so \(\left\lVert\Delta\mathbf{r}\right\rVert \lt \Delta s_k\text{;}\) but as the two points close in, the piece of curve between them becomes indistinguishable from the straight chord, in the sense that their ratio tends to \(1\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} \frac{\Delta s_k}{\left\lVert\Delta\mathbf{r}\right\rVert} \amp \ \longrightarrow\ 1 ,\\ \text{so} \quad \Delta s_k \amp \ \longrightarrow\ ds\\ \amp = \left\lVert d\mathbf{r}\right\rVert = \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt . \end{align*}
    🔗
    That last equality is exactly the substitution that makes a line integral computable, and it is what (1.3.5) records: trading the geometric element \(ds\) for \(\left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert \,dt\) is the same move as replacing the arc by its chord in Figure 1.3.10 and then shrinking it.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.3.11. Units.

    If we use meters and kilograms as the units of length and mass respectively, then what will the units of \(\rho(x,y,z)\) and \(ds\) be?
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(ds\) is a length, so it is measured in meters (\(\text{m}\)), and \(\rho\) is a mass per unit length, so it is measured in kilograms per meter (\(\text{kg}/\text{m}\)). The product \(\rho\,ds\) is then measured in kilograms, as a contribution to a mass must be.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.3.12. What is being integrated?

    In the above applications of line integrals of scalar functions \(\int_C f(x,y,z)\,ds\text{,}\) what is the scalar function that is being integrated (the integrand) along the wire?
    🔗
    Answer.
    For the mass (1.3.19) the integrand is the density, \(f = \rho\text{.}\) For the length (1.3.18) the integrand is the constant function \(f = 1\text{:}\) the length is the “mass” of a wire of density one.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.3.14. A mass with no parametrization.

    Assume that the density of the circular wire in Figure 1.3.13 is given by \(\rho(x,y) = \dfrac{1}{x^2+y^2+1}\text{.}\) Without parametrizing, find its mass.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(M = \dfrac{4\pi}{5} \approx 2.51\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Every point of the wire lies on the circle of radius \(2\text{,}\) so \(x^2+y^2 = 4\) along the curve, and the density is not really varying at all:
    \begin{equation*} \rho(x,y) = \frac{1}{x^2+y^2+1} = \frac{1}{4+1} = \frac{1}{5} \qquad \text{at every point of } C . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A constant comes out of the integral, and what is left is the length (1.3.18) of the wire, namely the circumference \(2\pi(2) = 4\pi\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} M \amp = \int_C \rho \, ds = \frac{1}{5}\int_C ds\\ \amp = \frac{1}{5}\left(4\pi\right) = \frac{4\pi}{5} \approx 2.51 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    This is the one situation in which a line integral needs no parametrization: when the integrand is constant on the curve, the integral is that constant times the length.

    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.3.15. Does the radius matter?

    Assume that the mass density of a circular wire of radius \(R\) centered at the origin is given by \(\rho(x,y) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\text{.}\) Which statement is correct?
    🔗
    1. The mass of the wire is independent of its radius \(R\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The mass of the wire increases as \(R\) increases.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The mass of the wire decreases as \(R\) decreases.
      🔗
      🔗
    Answer.
    Only option A.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    On the circle of radius \(R\) we have \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2} = R\text{,}\) so \(\rho = 1/R\) is constant along the wire and
    \begin{equation*} M = \int_C \rho\, ds = \frac{1}{R}\int_C ds = \frac{1}{R}\left(2\pi R\right) = 2\pi , \end{equation*}
    🔗
    independently of \(R\text{.}\) Only option A is correct.

    Checkpoint 1.3.16. Which wire is heavier?

    Assume that the density of the square-shaped wire and of the circular wire graphed in Figure 1.3.13 is given by \(\rho(x,y) = x^2+y^2\text{.}\) Then, without parametrizing the curves, choose the correct option below. Which statement is correct?
    🔗
    1. The circular wire is heavier.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The square-shaped wire is heavier.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The two wires have the same mass.
      🔗
      🔗
    Answer.
    Only option A.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The circle. Here \(\rho(x,y) = x^2+y^2\) is the square of the distance to the origin, and on a circle of radius \(R\) centered at the origin that distance never changes. So \(\rho = R^2\) is constant along the wire, it comes out of the integral, and what is left is the circumference:
    \begin{align*} M_{\text{circle}} \amp = \int_{\text{circle}} \left(x^2+y^2\right) ds = \int_{\text{circle}} R^2 \, ds\\ \amp = R^2 \left(2\pi R\right) = 2\pi R^3\\ \amp = 2\pi (2)^3 = 16\pi \approx 50.265 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    The square. We do not need its mass exactly—an upper bound is enough. The square is inscribed in the circle, so every one of its points lies inside or on the circle, which is to say
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2 \le R^2 \qquad \text{on the square.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Replacing the integrand by that upper bound, and using that the square has side \(R\sqrt{2} = 2\sqrt{2}\) and hence perimeter \(\int_{\text{square}} ds = 8\sqrt{2}\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} M_{\text{square}} \amp \lt \int_{\text{square}} R^2 \, ds = R^2 \int_{\text{square}} ds\\ \amp = (2)^2 \left(8\sqrt{2}\right) = 32\sqrt{2} \approx 45.255 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    The inequality is strict because \(x^2+y^2 \lt R^2\) everywhere on the square except at its four vertices. So the square weighs less than \(45.3\) while the circle weighs \(50.3\text{:}\) the circular wire is heavier, and neither integral had to be parametrized. Only option A is correct.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.3.17. A charged wire.

    Assume that the charge density of the square-shaped wire and of the circular wire graphed in Figure 1.3.13 is given by \(\rho(x,y) = \left|x\right| - \left|y\right|\text{.}\) Then, without parametrizing the curves, choose the correct option(s) below. Select all that apply.
    🔗
    1. The circular wire is negatively charged.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The square-shaped wire is positively charged.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. In both cases (square and circular wires) the amount of positive charge is equal to the amount of negative charge.
      🔗
      🔗
    Answer.
    Option C is correct.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let us focus on the quarter of each wire lying where \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\text{,}\) so that the absolute values disappear and
    \begin{equation*} \rho(x,y) = \left|x\right| - \left|y\right| = x - y . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The line \(y = x\) cuts that quarter into two halves, as in Figure 1.3.18. Below the line we have \(x \gt y\text{,}\) so \(\rho \gt 0\) there and the wire carries positive charge; above it \(x \lt y\text{,}\) so \(\rho \lt 0\) and the charge is negative.
    🔗
    Now reflect in the line \(y=x\text{,}\) that is, interchange \(x\) and \(y\text{.}\) This reflection carries each of the two shapes onto itself and it does not change lengths, so it matches the lower half of the quarter with the upper half piece for piece. On corresponding pieces the density is exactly opposite,
    \begin{equation*} \rho(y,x) = y - x = -\left(x-y\right) = -\rho(x,y) , \end{equation*}
    🔗
    so the positive charge on the half below the line is exactly cancelled by the negative charge on the half above it: this quarter of each wire is neutral. The same argument applies to the other three quarters, where the line of reflection is \(y = \pm x\) and the density is again \(\pm(|x|-|y|)\text{.}\) Adding up the four quarters,
    \begin{equation*} \int_C \left(\left|x\right| - \left|y\right|\right) ds = 0 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    for both the square and the circle. Neither wire is charged overall: each carries exactly as much positive as negative charge. Option C is correct.
