    Section 2.8 Applications: Mass and Center of Mass

    Multiple integrals let us compute physical quantities of solid objects and flat plates whose density varies from point to point. In this section we use double and triple integrals to compute the total mass of an object and to locate its center of mass—the point at which the object would balance perfectly. Before we study continuous objects, we review what the center of mass of a system of point masses is and why it deserves its name: first for two masses, then for any number of them. A flat plate and a solid are then handled in the same way, by cutting them into many small pieces and treating each piece as a point mass.
    🔗
    Starred material is optional. In this section that is Subsection 2.8.4, on point masses in space and solid objects.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.8.1 The Center of Mass of Two Point Masses

    Consider two masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) located at the points \(\mathbf{r}_1 = (x_1, y_1)\) and \(\mathbf{r}_2 = (x_2, y_2)\text{,}\) as in Figure 2.8.1, and let \(\mathbf{F}_1\) and \(\mathbf{F}_2\) be the net forces acting on them. By Newton’s second law, each force is the mass times the acceleration, \(\mathbf{F}_1 = m_1\, \dfrac{d^2\mathbf{r}_1}{dt^2}\) and \(\mathbf{F}_2 = m_2\, \dfrac{d^2\mathbf{r}_2}{dt^2}\text{,}\) so the total force on the system of the two masses is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2 = m_1 \frac{d^2\mathbf{r}_1}{dt^2} + m_2 \frac{d^2\mathbf{r}_2}{dt^2} = \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\left( \sum_{i=1}^{2} m_i\, \mathbf{r}_i \right). \end{equation*}
    It would be nice to rewrite this in the familiar form \(\mathbf{F} = M\mathbf{a}\) of Newton’s second law. To this end, we multiply and divide the right-hand side by the total mass \(M = m_1 + m_2\) of the system:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} = M\, \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\left( \sum_{i=1}^{2} \frac{m_i}{M}\, \mathbf{r}_i \right) = M\, \frac{d^2\bar{\mathbf{r}}}{dt^2}, \end{equation*}
    where we have defined the center of mass of the system as the point
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{\mathbf{r}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{2} m_i\, \mathbf{r}_i = \left( \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2},\ \frac{m_1 y_1 + m_2 y_2}{m_1 + m_2} \right).\tag{2.8.1} \end{equation}
    Thus the total force equals the total mass times the acceleration of a virtual mass \(M\) located at \(\bar{\mathbf{r}}\text{:}\) as far as the net force is concerned, the pair of masses moves like a single particle of mass \(M\) sitting at the center of mass. The formula is a weighted average of the two positions, with the masses as the weights. If \(m_1 = m_2\text{,}\) the center of mass is the midpoint of the segment joining the two masses; otherwise it lies on that segment, closer to the heavier mass.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.8.2 The Center of Mass of \(n\) Point Masses and the First Moments

    Nothing in the computation above depended on there being only two masses. For a system of \(n\) point masses \(m_1, \dots, m_n\) at the points \(\mathbf{r}_1, \dots, \mathbf{r}_n\text{,}\) adding Newton’s second law for each mass and multiplying and dividing by the total mass \(M = m_1 + \cdots + m_n\) gives, exactly as before, \(\mathbf{F} = M\, \dfrac{d^2\bar{\mathbf{r}}}{dt^2}\) with
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{\mathbf{r}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, \mathbf{r}_i, \qquad M = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i .\tag{2.8.2} \end{equation}
    The sums in the coordinates of \(\bar{\mathbf{r}}\) have names of their own. For masses in the plane, with \(\mathbf{r}_i = (x_i, y_i)\text{,}\) the first moments of the system are
    🔗
    \begin{equation} M_y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, x_i, \qquad M_x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, y_i .\tag{2.8.3} \end{equation}
    \(M_y\) is the moment about the \(y\)-axis: each mass is multiplied by its signed distance \(x_i\) from the \(y\)-axis, so \(M_y\) measures the tendency of the system to tip about that axis (masses to the right of the axis, with \(x_i \gt 0\text{,}\) count positively, and masses to its left count negatively). Likewise \(M_x\) is the moment about the \(x\)-axis, computed with the signed distances \(y_i\) from the \(x\)-axis. In this notation, (2.8.2) says that the center of mass \(\bar{\mathbf{r}} = (\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) has the coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M}.\tag{2.8.4} \end{equation}
    Every formula for a flat plate in the next subsection is one of these, with the sum over point masses replaced by an integral over the plate. Masses in space, and solid objects, are treated in Subsection 2.8.4.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.8.3 Finding the Center of Mass of a 2D Object

    Now let the mass be spread continuously over a flat plate that occupies a region \(R\) in the plane, with density \(\delta(x,y)\) (mass per unit area) at the point \((x,y)\text{.}\) To bring the formulas of the previous subsection to bear, we cut the plate into small pieces and treat each piece as a point mass. Partition the plane into small rectangles by the lines \(x = x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n\) and \(y = y_0, y_1, \dots, y_m\text{,}\) as in Figure 2.8.2. The rectangle in column \(i\) and row \(j\) has width \(\Delta x_i\text{,}\) height \(\Delta y_j\text{,}\) and area \(\Delta A_{ij} = \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j\text{;}\) if it meets \(R\text{,}\) we pick a point \((x_i, y_j)\) in it. The density is nearly constant on such a small piece, so the mass of the piece is approximately
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta m_{ij} = \delta(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta A_{ij} = \delta(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j . \end{equation*}
    The plate is now approximated by a system of point masses \(\Delta m_{ij}\) located at the points \((x_i, y_j)\text{,}\) one for each rectangle that meets \(R\text{.}\) By (2.8.2) and (2.8.3), the total mass and the first moments of this system are the double sums
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M \amp\approx \sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j,\\ M_y \amp\approx \sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\, \delta(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j, \qquad M_x \approx \sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_j\, \delta(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j, \end{align*}
    where the sums run over the rectangles that meet \(R\text{.}\) These are Riemann sums. Letting the rectangles shrink, that is, letting \(m, n \to \infty\text{,}\) turns each double sum into a double integral over \(R\text{.}\) For the mass,
    🔗
    \begin{equation} M = \lim_{m,n \to \infty} \sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j = \iint_R \delta(x,y)\, dA,\tag{2.8.5} \end{equation}
    and the first moments become double integrals in the same way. So the mass and the first moments of the plate are
    🔗
    \begin{equation} M = \iint_R \delta\, dA, \qquad M_y = \iint_R x\, \delta\, dA, \qquad M_x = \iint_R y\, \delta\, dA,\tag{2.8.6} \end{equation}
    where, as before, \(M_y\) is the moment about the \(y\)-axis and \(M_x\) the moment about the \(x\)-axis, and the center of mass is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M}.\tag{2.8.7} \end{equation}

    Definition 2.8.3.

    When the density of a plate or of a solid object is constant, the center of mass is called the centroid of the object.
    🔗
    🔗
    A constant density \(\delta\) cancels in the quotients of (2.8.7), so the centroid depends only on the shape of the object: to find a centroid we may as well take \(\delta = 1\text{,}\) and then the mass \(M\) is simply the area of the plate.
    🔗

    Example 2.8.4. Centroid of the Region Between Two Curves.

    Find the centroid of the region between \(y = \sqrt{x}\) and \(y = x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 1.
    The two curves intersect where \(\sqrt{x} = x^2\text{,}\) that is, at \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\text{,}\) so the region lies over the interval \(0 \le x \le 1\) with \(x^2 \le y \le \sqrt{x}\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 2.8.6. Since we are finding the centroid, we may take the constant density to be \(\delta = 1\text{,}\) in which case, by (2.8.6), the mass is simply the area:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} M = \int_0^1 \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} dy\, dx = \int_0^1 \left( \sqrt{x} - x^2 \right) dx = \left[ \frac{2}{3}x^{3/2} - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{3}. \end{equation*}
    By (2.8.6), the moment about the \(y\)-axis is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} M_y = \int_0^1 \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} x\, dy\, dx = \int_0^1 x \left( \sqrt{x} - x^2 \right) dx = \int_0^1 \left( x^{3/2} - x^3 \right) dx = \frac{2}{5} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{20}, \end{equation*}
    and the moment about the \(x\)-axis is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} M_x = \int_0^1 \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} y\, dy\, dx = \int_0^1 \frac{(\sqrt{x})^2 - (x^2)^2}{2}\, dx = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^1 \left( x - x^4 \right) dx = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{5} \right) = \frac{3}{20}. \end{equation*}
    By (2.8.7),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M} = \frac{3/20}{1/3} = \frac{9}{20}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M} = \frac{3/20}{1/3} = \frac{9}{20}, \end{equation*}
    so the centroid is \(\left( \tfrac{9}{20}, \tfrac{9}{20} \right)\text{.}\) It is no accident that \(\bar{x} = \bar{y}\text{:}\) the region is symmetric about the line \(y = x\text{,}\) so its centroid must lie on that line. See Figure 2.8.5 for an animated walkthrough.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.8.7. Centroid of a Cardioid.

    Find the centroid of the object that has the shape \(r = 1 + \cos(\theta)\) and density \(\delta = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution 2.
    The cardioid \(r = 1 + \cos(\theta)\) is shown in Figure 2.8.9. The density is constant, so we are indeed finding a centroid, and the answer will not depend on the value \(\delta = 2\text{.}\) The region is symmetric about the \(x\)-axis (replacing \(\theta\) by \(-\theta\) does not change \(r\)), so \(\bar{y} = 0\) and we only need \(\bar{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Working in polar coordinates with \(dA = r\, dr\, d\theta\) from (2.4.1), the mass (2.8.6) is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M \amp= \iint_R \delta\, dA = 2 \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{1+\cos\theta} r\, dr\, d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} (1 + \cos\theta)^2\, d\theta\\ \amp= \int_0^{2\pi} \left( 1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta \right) d\theta = 2\pi + 0 + \pi = 3\pi. \end{align*}
    Since \(x = r\cos\theta\) by (2.3.1), the moment about the \(y\)-axis is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M_y \amp= \iint_R x\, \delta\, dA = 2 \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{1+\cos\theta} (r\cos\theta)\, r\, dr\, d\theta = \frac{2}{3} \int_0^{2\pi} \cos\theta\, (1 + \cos\theta)^3\, d\theta\\ \amp= \frac{2}{3} \int_0^{2\pi} \left( \cos\theta + 3\cos^2\theta + 3\cos^3\theta + \cos^4\theta \right) d\theta = \frac{2}{3} \left( 0 + 3\pi + 0 + \frac{3\pi}{4} \right) = \frac{5\pi}{2}, \end{align*}
    where the odd powers of cosine integrate to zero over a full period, \(\int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2\theta\, d\theta = \pi\text{,}\) and \(\int_0^{2\pi} \cos^4\theta\, d\theta = \tfrac{3\pi}{4}\text{.}\) By (2.8.7),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M} = \frac{5\pi/2}{3\pi} = \frac{5}{6}, \end{equation*}
    so the centroid is \(\left( \tfrac{5}{6}, 0 \right)\text{.}\) As promised, the constant density \(\delta = 2\) canceled out. The animation in Figure 2.8.8 shows how symmetry and the polar integrals combine to locate this point.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.8.4 Finding the Center of Mass of a 3D Object*

    Everything so far took place in the plane. For point masses in space, with \(\mathbf{r}_i = (x_i, y_i, z_i)\) in (2.8.2), the distances are measured from the coordinate planes instead of the coordinate axes. The first moments about the coordinate planes are
    🔗
    \begin{equation} M_{yz} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, x_i, \qquad M_{xz} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, y_i, \qquad M_{xy} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, z_i\tag{2.8.8} \end{equation}
    (\(M_{yz}\) uses the signed distance \(x_i\) from the \(yz\)-plane, and so on), and the center of mass \(\bar{\mathbf{r}} = (\bar{x}, \bar{y}, \bar{z})\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{x} = \frac{M_{yz}}{M}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{M_{xz}}{M}, \qquad \bar{z} = \frac{M_{xy}}{M}.\tag{2.8.9} \end{equation}
    Now let a solid object occupy a region \(D\) in space, with density \(\delta(x,y,z)\) (mass per unit volume) at the point \((x,y,z)\text{.}\) The plan is the same as for a plate, one dimension up: we cut the solid into small boxes and treat each box as a point mass. Partition space into boxes by the planes \(x = x_0, \dots, x_n\text{,}\) \(y = y_0, \dots, y_m\text{,}\) and \(z = z_0, \dots, z_p\text{.}\) The box in position \(i\) along the \(x\)-direction, \(j\) along the \(y\)-direction, and \(k\) along the \(z\)-direction has the dimensions \(\Delta x_i\text{,}\) \(\Delta y_j\text{,}\) \(\Delta z_k\) and the volume \(\Delta V_{ijk} = \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j\, \Delta z_k\text{;}\) if it meets \(D\text{,}\) we pick a point \((x_i, y_j, z_k)\) in it, as in Figure 2.8.11. The density is nearly constant on so small a box, so its mass is approximately
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta m_{ijk} = \delta(x_i, y_j, z_k)\, \Delta V_{ijk} = \delta(x_i, y_j, z_k)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j\, \Delta z_k . \end{equation*}
    The solid is approximated by the system of point masses \(\Delta m_{ijk}\) at the points \((x_i, y_j, z_k)\text{,}\) one for each box that meets \(D\text{,}\) so (2.8.2) and (2.8.8) give its mass and its first moments as triple sums; for instance,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M \amp\approx \sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta(x_i, y_j, z_k)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j\, \Delta z_k ,\\ M_{xy} \amp\approx \sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n} z_k\, \delta(x_i, y_j, z_k)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j\, \Delta z_k , \end{align*}
    the sums running over the boxes that meet \(D\text{.}\) (Newton’s second law for this system of boxes reads \(\mathbf{F} = M\, \dfrac{d^2\bar{\mathbf{r}}}{dt^2}\text{,}\) as in the previous subsections: the solid moves as if its whole mass were concentrated at its center of mass.) These are Riemann sums, and letting the boxes shrink, \(n, m, p \to \infty\text{,}\) turns each triple sum into a triple integral over \(D\text{.}\) For the mass,
    🔗
    \begin{equation} M = \lim_{n,m,p \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta(x_i, y_j, z_k)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j\, \Delta z_k = \iiint_D \delta(x,y,z)\, dV.\tag{2.8.10} \end{equation}
    Watch the construction unfold in Figure 2.8.10.
    🔗
    In the same way, the first moments become the triple integrals
    🔗
    \begin{equation} M_{yz} = \iiint_D x\, \delta\, dV, \qquad M_{xz} = \iiint_D y\, \delta\, dV, \qquad M_{xy} = \iiint_D z\, \delta\, dV,\tag{2.8.11} \end{equation}
    the moments about the \(yz\)-, \(xz\)-, and \(xy\)-planes, and the center of mass \((\bar{x}, \bar{y}, \bar{z})\) of the solid is the point whose coordinates are the moments divided by the total mass:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{x} = \frac{M_{yz}}{M}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{M_{xz}}{M}, \qquad \bar{z} = \frac{M_{xy}}{M}.\tag{2.8.12} \end{equation}

    Example 2.8.12. Center of Mass of a Solid Bounded by a Paraboloid.

    Find the center of mass of a solid of constant density \(\delta\text{,}\) which is bounded by the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\) (in the plane \(z = 0\)) and the paraboloid \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The solid is shown in Figure 2.8.14. Both the region and the density are symmetric about the \(z\)-axis, so the center of mass lies on that axis: \(\bar{x} = \bar{y} = 0\text{.}\) It remains to find \(\bar{z} = M_{xy}/M\) from (2.8.12).
    🔗
    Because of the circular symmetry, we work in cylindrical coordinates, where (2.6.2) applies and the region is described by \(0 \le z \le 4 - r^2\text{,}\) \(0 \le r \le 2\text{,}\) and \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\text{.}\) By (2.8.10), the mass is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M \amp= \iiint_D \delta\, dV = \delta \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 \int_0^{4-r^2} dz\, r\, dr\, d\theta\\ \amp= \delta \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 (4 - r^2)\, r\, dr\, d\theta = \delta \int_0^{2\pi} \left[ 2r^2 - \frac{r^4}{4} \right]_0^2 d\theta\\ \amp= \delta \int_0^{2\pi} 4\, d\theta = 8\pi\delta. \end{align*}
    Using (2.8.11), the first moment about the \(xy\)-plane is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M_{xy} \amp= \iiint_D z\, \delta\, dV = \delta \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 \int_0^{4-r^2} z\, dz\, r\, dr\, d\theta\\ \amp= \delta \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^2 \frac{(4 - r^2)^2}{2}\, r\, dr\, d\theta = \delta \int_0^{2\pi} \left[ -\frac{(4-r^2)^3}{12} \right]_0^2 d\theta\\ \amp= \delta \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{16}{3}\, d\theta = \frac{32\pi\delta}{3}. \end{align*}
    Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \bar{z} = \frac{M_{xy}}{M} = \frac{32\pi\delta/3}{8\pi\delta} = \frac{4}{3}, \end{equation*}
    and the center of mass is \(\left(0, 0, \tfrac{4}{3}\right)\text{:}\) since the density is constant, this point is the centroid of the solid. Notice that the answer does not depend on \(\delta\text{,}\) as a centroid never does: for an object of constant density, the location of the balance point is determined by the shape alone. Watch the computation come together in Figure 2.8.13.

    Subsection 2.8.5 Concept Check

    The first problem is about point masses, where everything can be done by hand in a line or two; the second revisits the region of Example 2.8.4 with two densities that are not constant.

    Checkpoint 2.8.15. Three point masses.

    Masses of \(1\text{,}\) \(2\text{,}\) and \(3\) kilograms sit at the points \((0,0)\text{,}\) \((4,0)\text{,}\) and \((0,4)\text{,}\) respectively. Find the center of mass of the system. Where would it be if the three masses were equal?
    🔗
    Answer.
    By (2.8.3) and (2.8.4), with \(M = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} M_y = 1\cdot 0 + 2\cdot 4 + 3\cdot 0 = 8, \qquad M_x = 1\cdot 0 + 2\cdot 0 + 3\cdot 4 = 12, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \bar{x} = \frac{8}{6} = \frac{4}{3}, \quad \bar{y} = \frac{12}{6} = 2 . \end{equation*}
    The center of mass \(\left(\tfrac{4}{3}, 2\right)\) lies inside the triangle with the masses at its vertices, pulled toward the heaviest mass at \((0,4)\text{:}\) \(\bar{y} = 2\) exceeds \(\bar{x} = 4/3\) because the mass at \((0,4)\) is heavier than the one at \((4,0)\text{.}\) With three equal masses \(m\text{,}\) every weight \(m_i/M\) in (2.8.2) is \(1/3\text{,}\) and the center of mass is the average of the three positions, \(\left( \tfrac{0+4+0}{3}, \tfrac{0+0+4}{3} \right) = \left( \tfrac{4}{3}, \tfrac{4}{3} \right)\text{:}\) the centroid of the triangle, where its three medians meet.

    Checkpoint 2.8.16. The same plate, two densities.

    Find the center of mass of the region between \(y = \sqrt{x}\) and \(y = x^2\text{,}\) assuming that the density function is
    🔗
    1. \(\delta_1(x,y) = xy\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\delta_2(x,y) = y\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Question: Do your answers in parts A and B match your intuition? If so, justify your answer in each case. (For comparison, the centroid of this region is \(\left(\tfrac{9}{20}, \tfrac{9}{20}\right)\text{,}\) by Example 2.8.4.)
    🔗
    Answer.
    The region is \(0 \le x \le 1\text{,}\) \(x^2 \le y \le \sqrt{x}\text{,}\) and we use (2.8.6) and (2.8.7).
    🔗
    A. With \(\delta_1 = xy\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M \amp= \int_0^1 \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} xy\, dy\, dx = \int_0^1 \frac{x\,(x - x^4)}{2}\, dx = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{6} \right) = \frac{1}{12},\\ M_y \amp= \int_0^1 \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} x^2 y\, dy\, dx = \int_0^1 \frac{x^2\,(x - x^4)}{2}\, dx = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{7} \right) = \frac{3}{56},\\ M_x \amp= \int_0^1 \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} x y^2\, dy\, dx = \int_0^1 \frac{x\,(x^{3/2} - x^6)}{3}\, dx = \frac{1}{3}\left( \frac{2}{7} - \frac{1}{8} \right) = \frac{3}{56}, \end{align*}
    so \(\bar{x} = \bar{y} = \dfrac{3/56}{1/12} = \dfrac{9}{14} \approx 0.64\text{.}\) This fits: the density \(xy\) is largest near the corner \((1,1)\) of the region and vanishes at the origin, so the balance point moves from the centroid \((9/20, 9/20)\) toward \((1,1)\text{;}\) and it stays on the line \(y = x\text{,}\) because swapping \(x\) and \(y\) changes neither the region nor the density \(xy\text{.}\)
    🔗
    B. With \(\delta_2 = y\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} M \amp= \int_0^1 \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} y\, dy\, dx = \int_0^1 \frac{x - x^4}{2}\, dx = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{5} \right) = \frac{3}{20},\\ M_y \amp= \int_0^1 \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} xy\, dy\, dx = \frac{1}{12} \quad\text{(the mass of part A)},\\ M_x \amp= \int_0^1 \int_{x^2}^{\sqrt{x}} y^2\, dy\, dx = \int_0^1 \frac{x^{3/2} - x^6}{3}\, dx = \frac{1}{3}\left( \frac{2}{5} - \frac{1}{7} \right) = \frac{3}{35}, \end{align*}
    so \(\bar{x} = \dfrac{1/12}{3/20} = \dfrac{5}{9} \approx 0.56\) and \(\bar{y} = \dfrac{3/35}{3/20} = \dfrac{4}{7} \approx 0.57\text{.}\) This fits too: the density grows with \(y\text{,}\) so the mass sits higher than in a uniform plate, and \(\bar{y}\) rises from \(9/20\) to \(4/7\text{.}\) The center of mass now lies slightly above the line \(y = x\text{:}\) the density \(y\) treats \(x\) and \(y\) differently, so nothing forces the balance point onto that line. (\(\bar{x}\) rises as well, from \(9/20\) to \(5/9\text{,}\) because the heavier upper part of this region is also its right part.)
