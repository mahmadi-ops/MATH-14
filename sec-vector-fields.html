    Section 4 Vector Fields and Line Integrals

    In this section we meet two ideas. A vector field attaches a vector—a magnitude and a direction—to every point of the plane or of space; think of the velocity of the air at each point of a room, or the gravitational pull at each point around a planet. The line integral of a vector field then answers a natural question: as an object moves along a curve through the field, how much does the field push it along its way? The physical name for this accumulated push is work.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1 Vector Fields

    What is a vector field? We will study the definition through an example. Assume that we have a function \(\mathbf{F}(x,y)\) on \(\R^2\text{:}\) the function \(\mathbf{F}\) takes two real numbers \((x,y)\) as its input and outputs a vector in \(\R^2\text{.}\) As an example, consider the following vector field:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}(x,y)=x\,\mathbf{i}+y\,\mathbf{j}. \end{equation*}
    To visualize this vector field, imagine that at each point \((x,y)\) there is a vector \(x\,\mathbf{i}+y\,\mathbf{j}\) attached. Physically, this vector could correspond to the velocity of an atom located at the point \((x,y)\text{.}\) See Figure 4.1.
    🔗
    Note that the length of the vector \(x\,\mathbf{i}+y\,\mathbf{j}\) is \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\text{,}\) the distance from the origin. This is why the blue arrows in Figure 4.1 grow in length as we move away from the origin along a straight line. Can we modify the vector field so that the length of the vectors is one at every point? The answer is yes: dividing the vector by its own length produces a unit vector, and hence the vector field
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}(x,y)=\frac{x\,\mathbf{i}+y\,\mathbf{j}}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}, \end{equation*}
    when visualized, looks like Figure 4.2: the directions are unchanged, but now all arrows have the same length.
    🔗
    Our next example is a vector field which is a function on \(\R^3\text{.}\) Consider the following vector field:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) =\frac{-x\,\mathbf{i}-y\,\mathbf{j}-z\,\mathbf{k}}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}} =-\frac{\mathbf{r}}{\left|\mathbf{r}\right|^3}, \qquad \mathbf{r}=x\,\mathbf{i}+y\,\mathbf{j}+z\,\mathbf{k}. \end{equation*}
    This can, for instance, represent the gravitational field of a massive body located at the origin: every arrow points toward the origin, and since \(\left|\mathbf{F}\right| =\left|\mathbf{r}\right|/\left|\mathbf{r}\right|^3 =1/\left|\mathbf{r}\right|^2\text{,}\) the pull weakens with the square of the distance—the inverse-square law. See Figure 4.3. We will meet this field again in Example 4.9.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.2 Work (Reminder)

    Our goal is to study the line integral of a vector field over a curve \(C\text{,}\) and we will do so using an example from physics: calculating the amount of work done by a force \(\mathbf{F}\text{,}\) which in general can be a function of \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(z\text{.}\) Before we present the example, let us remind ourselves of the definition of work. Consider a box being pulled by a constant force \(\mathbf{F}\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 4.4.
    🔗
    The amount of work done by the force is equal to the magnitude of the force in the direction of motion, \(\left|\mathbf{F}\right|\cos(\alpha)\text{,}\) multiplied by the amount of displacement \(\delta x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \text{Work} =\left|\mathbf{F}\right|\cos(\alpha)\times\delta x =\mathbf{F}\cdot\left(\delta x\,\mathbf{i}\right). \end{equation*}
    The last equality holds because the motion is in the \(+x\)-direction, which lets us rewrite \(\left|\mathbf{F}\right|\cos(\alpha)\times\delta x\) as the dot product \(\mathbf{F}\cdot(\delta x\,\mathbf{i})\text{.}\) This dot-product form is the one that generalizes: work is the dot product of the force with the displacement.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.3 Work of a Force Field

    Let us now consider the situation where a force \(\mathbf{F}(x,y)\) is acting on an object and causing it to move along a curved trajectory from an initial point to a final point. Neither the force nor the direction of motion is constant anymore, so we cannot apply the formula above directly. Instead, we reuse the strategy of Section 3: calculate the work on small subarcs of the trajectory, where force and direction are nearly constant, and then sum up. Figure 4.5 shows one such subarc.
    🔗
    On the \(k\)th subarc the displacement has length \(\Delta s_k\) and direction \(\mathbf{T}_k\text{,}\) the unit tangent vector, so by the dot-product formula for work, the work done along the \(k\)th subarc is \(\mathbf{F}(x_k,y_k)\cdot\mathbf{T}_k\,\Delta s_k\text{.}\) Therefore the work done over the whole curve can be approximated as
    \begin{equation*} W\approx\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{F}(x_k,y_k)\cdot\mathbf{T}_k\,\Delta s_k. \end{equation*}
    Finally, to find the exact amount of work we take the limit as the number of subarcs approaches infinity:
    \begin{equation*} W=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{F}(x_k,y_k)\cdot\mathbf{T}_k\,\Delta s_k =\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds, \end{equation*}
    where \(\mathbf{T}\) is the unit tangent vector of the curve. Since
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{T}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\left|\mathbf{v}\right|} =\frac{\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}}{\frac{ds}{dt}} =\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds}, \end{equation*}
    the combination \(\mathbf{T}\,ds\) is simply \(d\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) and we arrive at the compact form
    \begin{equation*} W=\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds =\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In practice, once the curve is parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) for \(a\le t\le b\text{,}\) the recipe is
    \begin{equation*} \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =\int_a^b \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t)\bigr)\cdot \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}\,dt =\int_a^b \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t)\bigr)\cdot\mathbf{v}(t)\,dt: \end{equation*}
    evaluate the field along the curve, dot it with the velocity, and integrate over time. Work was our motivation, but any vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) can be integrated over a curve \(C\) in this fashion. Figure 4.6 shows the accumulation of \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds\) along a trajectory.
    🔗
    There is a third, frequently used way to write the same integral. Assume the vector field has components \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z)=M(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{i}+N(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{j} +P(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{k}\text{.}\) Since \(d\mathbf{r}=dx\,\mathbf{i}+dy\,\mathbf{j}+dz\,\mathbf{k}\text{,}\) expanding the dot product gives the differential form of the line integral:
    \begin{equation*} \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =\int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz. \end{equation*}
    All three notations—\(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds\text{,}\) \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) and \(\int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz\)—mean exactly the same thing.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.7. Work along a twisted cubic.

    Calculate the work done by the force
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}(x,y,z)=(y-x^2)\,\mathbf{i}+(z-y^2)\,\mathbf{j} +(x-z^2)\,\mathbf{k} \end{equation*}
    over the curve \(C\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t)=t\,\mathbf{i}+t^2\,\mathbf{j}+t^3\,\mathbf{k} \end{equation*}
    from \((0,0,0)\) to \((1,1,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The curve, shown in Figure 4.8, runs from \(\mathbf{r}(0)=(0,0,0)\) to \(\mathbf{r}(1)=(1,1,1)\text{,}\) so the parameter interval is \(0\le t\le 1\text{.}\) We begin by writing the force in terms of the parameter \(t\text{,}\) substituting \(x=t\text{,}\) \(y=t^2\text{,}\) \(z=t^3\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{F} \amp = (y-x^2)\,\mathbf{i}+(z-y^2)\,\mathbf{j} +(x-z^2)\,\mathbf{k}\\ \amp = (t^2-t^2)\,\mathbf{i}+(t^3-t^4)\,\mathbf{j} +(t-t^6)\,\mathbf{k}\\ \amp = (t^3-t^4)\,\mathbf{j}+(t-t^6)\,\mathbf{k}, \end{align*}
    where the \(\mathbf{i}\)-component vanishes identically along this particular curve. The velocity vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}(t)=\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} =\mathbf{i}+2t\,\mathbf{j}+3t^2\,\mathbf{k}. \end{equation*}
    Finally, the amount of work is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} W \amp = \int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =\int_0^1\Bigl((t^3-t^4)\,\mathbf{j}+(t-t^6)\,\mathbf{k}\Bigr) \cdot\Bigl(\mathbf{i}+2t\,\mathbf{j}+3t^2\,\mathbf{k}\Bigr)\,dt\\ \amp = \int_0^1\left(2t^4-2t^5+3t^3-3t^8\right)dt =\frac{2}{5}-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{3} =\frac{29}{60}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.9. Work done by the gravitational field along a helix.

    Calculate the line integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_C \frac{-x}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dx +\frac{-y}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dy +\frac{-z}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dz \end{equation*}
    over the curve \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle\cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle\) from \((1,0,0)\) to \((1,0,2\pi)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This integral is in the differential form \(\int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz\) with \(\mathbf{F}=\langle M,N,P\rangle\text{,}\) and we recognize the field:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} =\frac{\langle -x,\,-y,\,-z\rangle}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}} =-\frac{\mathbf{r}}{\left|\mathbf{r}\right|^3}, \end{equation*}
    the gravitational field of Figure 4.3. The curve is a helix that climbs one full turn, from \((1,0,0)\) at \(t=0\) to \((1,0,2\pi)\) at \(t=2\pi\text{;}\) see Figure 4.10.
    🔗
    We write the field along the curve in terms of \(t\text{.}\) On the helix,
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2+z^2=\cos^2 t+\sin^2 t+t^2=1+t^2, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t)\bigr) =\frac{1}{\left(1+t^2\right)^{3/2}} \langle -\cos t,\ -\sin t,\ -t\rangle. \end{equation*}
    The velocity vector is \(\mathbf{v}=\langle -\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 1\rangle\text{,}\) and the dot product collapses nicely:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v} =\frac{\cos t\sin t-\sin t\cos t-t}{\left(1+t^2\right)^{3/2}} =\frac{-t}{\left(1+t^2\right)^{3/2}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore, using the substitution \(u=1+t^2\text{,}\) \(\tfrac{du}{2}=t\,dt\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz \amp = \int_{t=0}^{t=2\pi}\frac{-t}{\left(1+t^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dt = -\frac{1}{2}\int_{u=1}^{u=1+4\pi^2} u^{-3/2}\,du\\ \amp = \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)(-2)\, \frac{1}{\sqrt{u}}\Bigg|_{u=1}^{u=1+4\pi^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4\pi^2}}-1\approx -0.84. \end{align*}
    The work is negative, and the picture explains why: the gravitational field always points toward the origin, while the helix climbs steadily away from it, so the tangential component of the force opposes the motion at every instant. The accumulation of this negative work is animated in Figure 4.11.
