    Section 1.4 Vector Fields and Line Integrals

    In this section we meet two ideas. A vector field attaches a vector—a magnitude and a direction—to every point of the plane or of space; think of the velocity of the air at each point of a room, or the gravitational pull at each point around a planet. The line integral of a vector field then answers a natural question: as an object moves along a curve through the field, how much does the field push it along its way? The physical name for this accumulated push is work.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.1 Vector Fields

    What is a vector field? We will study the definition through an example. Assume that we have a function \(\mathbf{F}(x,y)\) on \(\R^2\text{:}\) the function \(\mathbf{F}\) takes two real numbers \((x,y)\) as its input and outputs a vector in \(\R^2\text{.}\) As an example, consider the following vector field:
    \begin{gather} \mathbf{F}(x,y)=\left\langle x,\ y\right\rangle.\tag{1.4.1} \end{gather}
    To visualize this vector field, imagine that at each point \((x,y)\) there is a vector \(\left\langle x,\ y\right\rangle\) attached. Physically, this vector could correspond to the velocity of an atom located at the point \((x,y)\text{.}\) See Figure 1.4.1.
    🔗
    Note that the length of the vector in (1.4.1) is \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\text{,}\) the distance from the origin. This is why the blue arrows in Figure 1.4.1 grow in length as we move away from the origin along a straight line. Can we modify the vector field so that the length of the vectors is one at every point? The answer is yes: dividing the vector by its own length produces a unit vector, and hence the vector field
    \begin{gather} \mathbf{F}(x,y) =\frac{\left\langle x,\ y\right\rangle}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},\tag{1.4.2} \end{gather}
    of (1.4.2), when visualized, looks like Figure 1.4.2: the directions are unchanged, but now all arrows have the same length.
    🔗
    Our next example is a vector field which is a function on \(\R^3\text{.}\) Consider the following vector field:
    \begin{align} \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) \amp = \frac{\left\langle -x,\ -y,\ -z\right\rangle} {\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\notag\\ \amp = -\frac{\mathbf{r}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert^3}, \qquad \mathbf{r}=\left\langle x,\ y,\ z\right\rangle.\tag{1.4.3} \end{align}
    This field has the same dependence on \(x\text{,}\) \(y\) and \(z\) as the gravitational force exerted by a massive body located at the origin.
     1 
    The gravitational force exerted by a mass \(m_1\) at the origin on a mass \(m_2\) at the point \(\mathbf{r}\) is the gravitational force field \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{grav}} = -\dfrac{Gm_1m_2}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert^3}\, \mathbf{r}\text{,}\) where \(G\) is the gravitational constant and the minus sign makes the force point back toward the origin. The field \(\mathbf{F}=-\mathbf{r}/\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert^3\) studied here is that force with the constant \(Gm_1m_2\) stripped off, so the two differ only by a positive factor and point the same way at every point.
    Every arrow points toward the origin, and since \(\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert =\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert/\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert^3 =1/\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert^2\text{,}\) the pull weakens with the square of the distance—the inverse-square law. See Figure 1.4.3. We will meet this field again in Example 1.4.10.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.2 Work (Reminder)

    Our goal is to study the line integral of a vector field over a curve \(C\text{,}\) and we will do so using an example from physics: calculating the amount of work done by a force \(\mathbf{F}\text{,}\) which in general can be a function of \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(z\text{.}\) Before we present the example, let us remind ourselves of the definition of work. Consider a box being pulled by a constant force \(\mathbf{F}\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 1.4.4.
    🔗
    The amount of work done by the force is equal to the magnitude of the force in the direction of motion, \(\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert\cos(\alpha)\text{,}\) multiplied by the amount of displacement \(\delta x\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather} \text{Work} =\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert\cos(\alpha)\times\delta x =\mathbf{F}\cdot\left(\delta x\,\mathbf{i}\right).\tag{1.4.4} \end{gather}
    The last equality holds because the motion is in the \(+x\)-direction, which lets us rewrite \(\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert\cos(\alpha)\times\delta x\) as the dot product \(\mathbf{F}\cdot(\delta x\,\mathbf{i})\text{.}\) This dot-product form is the one that generalizes: work is the dot product of the force with the displacement.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.3 Work of a Force Field

    Let us now consider the situation where a force \(\mathbf{F}(x,y)\) is acting on an object and causing it to move along a curved trajectory from an initial point to a final point. Neither the force nor the direction of motion is constant anymore, so we cannot apply (1.4.4) directly. Instead, we reuse the strategy of Section 1.3: calculate the work on small subarcs of the trajectory, where force and direction are nearly constant, and then sum up. Figure 1.4.5 shows one such subarc.
    🔗
    On the \(k\)th subarc the displacement has length \(\Delta s_k\) and direction \(\mathbf{T}_k\text{,}\) the unit tangent vector, so by the dot-product formula for work, the work done along the \(k\)th subarc is \(\mathbf{F}(x_k,y_k)\cdot\mathbf{T}_k\,\Delta s_k\text{.}\) Therefore the work done over the whole curve can be approximated as
    \begin{gather} W\approx\sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{F}(x_k,y_k)\cdot\mathbf{T}_k\,\Delta s_k.\tag{1.4.5} \end{gather}
    Finally, to find the exact amount of work we take the limit of (1.4.5) as the number of subarcs approaches infinity:
    \begin{align} W \amp =\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{F}(x_k,y_k)\cdot\mathbf{T}_k\,\Delta s_k\notag\\ \amp =\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds,\tag{1.4.6} \end{align}
    where \(\mathbf{T}\) is the unit tangent vector of the curve. Since
    \begin{gather} \mathbf{T}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} =\frac{\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}}{\frac{ds}{dt}} =\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds},\tag{1.4.7} \end{gather}
    the combination \(\mathbf{T}\,ds\) in (1.4.6) is simply \(d\mathbf{r}\) by (1.4.7), and we arrive at the compact form
    \begin{gather} W=\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds =\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}.\tag{1.4.8} \end{gather}
    🔗
    In practice, once the curve is parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) for \(a\le t\le b\text{,}\) (1.4.8) becomes
    \begin{align} \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} \amp =\int_a^b \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t)\bigr)\cdot \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}\,dt\notag\\ \amp =\int_a^b \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t)\bigr) \cdot\mathbf{v}(t)\,dt :\tag{1.4.9} \end{align}
    evaluate the field along the curve, dot it with the velocity, and integrate over time.
    🔗
    The applet below carries every ingredient of (1.4.9) at once: a curve \(C\) parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t)\text{,}\) the velocity \(\mathbf{v}(t)=d\mathbf{r}/dt\) tangent to it, and a force field \(\mathbf{F}\) evaluated at the moving point. Work out what the integrand \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) is doing before you compute anything.
    🔗
    Work was our motivation, but any vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) can be integrated over a curve \(C\) in this fashion; the number it produces is also called the circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) along \(C\text{,}\) a name that becomes the usual one when \(C\) is a closed curve. Figure 1.4.7 shows the accumulation of \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds\) along a trajectory.
    🔗
    There is a third, frequently used way to write the same integral. Assume the vector field has components \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z)=\left\langle M(x,y,z),\ N(x,y,z),\ P(x,y,z)\right\rangle\text{.}\) Since \(d\mathbf{r}=\left\langle dx,\ dy,\ dz\right\rangle\text{,}\) expanding the dot product gives the differential form of the line integral:
    \begin{gather} \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =\int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz.\tag{1.4.10} \end{gather}
    All three notations—(1.4.6), \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) and \(\int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz\)—mean exactly the same thing.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.8. Work along a twisted cubic.

    Calculate the work done by the force
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}(x,y,z)=\left\langle y-x^2,\ z-y^2,\ x-z^2\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    over the curve \(C\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t)=\left\langle t,\ t^2,\ t^3\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    from \((0,0,0)\) to \((1,1,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We use (1.4.9). The curve, shown in Figure 1.4.9, runs from \(\mathbf{r}(0)=\left\langle 0,0,0\right\rangle\) to \(\mathbf{r}(1)=\left\langle 1,1,1\right\rangle\text{,}\) so the parameter interval is \(0\le t\le 1\text{.}\) We begin by writing the force in terms of the parameter \(t\text{,}\) substituting \(x=t\text{,}\) \(y=t^2\text{,}\) \(z=t^3\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{F} \amp = \left\langle y-x^2,\ z-y^2,\ x-z^2\right\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle t^2-t^2,\ t^3-t^4,\ t-t^6\right\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle 0,\ t^3-t^4,\ t-t^6\right\rangle, \end{align*}
    where the first component vanishes identically along this particular curve. The velocity vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}(t)=\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} =\left\langle 1,\ 2t,\ 3t^2\right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    Finally, the amount of work is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} W \amp = \int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\\ \amp =\int_0^1\left\langle 0,\ t^3-t^4,\ t-t^6\right\rangle \cdot\left\langle 1,\ 2t,\ 3t^2\right\rangle\,dt\\ \amp = \int_0^1\left(2t^4-2t^5+3t^3-3t^8\right)dt\\ \amp = \frac{2}{5}-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{3} = \frac{29}{60}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.10. Work done by an inverse-square field along a helix.

    Calculate the line integral
    \begin{align*} \int_C \amp \frac{-x}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dx\\ \amp +\frac{-y}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dy\\ \amp +\frac{-z}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dz \end{align*}
    over the curve \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle\cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle\) from \((1,0,0)\) to \((1,0,2\pi)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    This integral is in the differential form (1.4.10) with \(\mathbf{F}=\langle M,N,P\rangle\text{,}\) and we recognize the field:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} =\frac{\langle -x,\,-y,\,-z\rangle}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}} =-\frac{\mathbf{r}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert^3}, \end{equation*}
    the inverse-square field (1.4.3), drawn in Figure 1.4.3, which varies with position just as the gravitational force does. The curve is a helix that climbs one full turn, from \((1,0,0)\) at \(t=0\) to \((1,0,2\pi)\) at \(t=2\pi\text{;}\) see Figure 1.4.11.
    🔗
    We write the field along the curve in terms of \(t\text{.}\) On the helix,
    \begin{equation*} x^2+y^2+z^2=\cos^2 t+\sin^2 t+t^2=1+t^2, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t)\bigr) =\frac{1}{\left(1+t^2\right)^{3/2}} \langle -\cos t,\ -\sin t,\ -t\rangle. \end{equation*}
    The velocity vector is \(\mathbf{v}=\langle -\sin t,\ \cos t,\ 1\rangle\text{,}\) and the dot product collapses nicely:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v} =\frac{\cos t\sin t-\sin t\cos t-t}{\left(1+t^2\right)^{3/2}} =\frac{-t}{\left(1+t^2\right)^{3/2}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore, using the substitution \(u=1+t^2\text{,}\) \(\tfrac{du}{2}=t\,dt\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align} \int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz \amp = \int_{t=0}^{t=2\pi}\frac{-t}{\left(1+t^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dt\notag\\ \amp = -\frac{1}{2}\int_{u=1}^{u=1+4\pi^2} u^{-3/2}\,du\notag\\ \amp = \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)(-2)\, \frac{1}{\sqrt{u}}\Bigg|_{u=1}^{u=1+4\pi^2}\notag\\ \amp = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4\pi^2}}-1\approx -0.84.\tag{1.4.11} \end{align}
    The work is negative, and the picture explains why: the field always points toward the origin, while the helix climbs steadily away from it, so the tangential component of the force opposes the motion at every instant. The accumulation of this negative work is animated in Figure 1.4.12.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.13. The same endpoints, two paths, two answers.

    Calculate the work done by the force
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}(x,y)=\left\langle y,\ 2x\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    in moving a particle from \(O(0,0)\) to \(P(1,1)\) along each of the following two paths:
    • \(C_1\text{,}\) the straight segment from \(O\) to \(P\text{,}\) parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}_1(t)=\left\langle t,\ t\right\rangle\text{,}\) \(0\le t\le 1\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(C_2\text{,}\) the arc of the parabola \(y=x^2\) from \(O\) to \(P\text{,}\) parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}_2(t)=\left\langle t,\ t^2\right\rangle\text{,}\) \(0\le t\le 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Both curves start at \(O(0,0)\) and end at \(P(1,1)\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 1.4.14, so any difference between the two answers can come only from the paths themselves. We use (1.4.9) for each one.
    🔗
    Along \(C_1\text{.}\) Here \(x=t\) and \(y=t\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{F}=\left\langle y,\ 2x\right\rangle =\left\langle t,\ 2t\right\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{r}_1'(t)=\left\langle 1,\ 1\right\rangle\text{.}\) Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{align*} W_1 \amp = \int_{C_1}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^1\left\langle t,\ 2t\right\rangle \cdot\left\langle 1,\ 1\right\rangle dt\\ \amp = \int_0^1 3t\,dt = \frac{3}{2}. \end{align*}
    Along \(C_2\text{.}\) Here \(x=t\) and \(y=t^2\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{F}=\left\langle y,\ 2x\right\rangle =\left\langle t^2,\ 2t\right\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{r}_2'(t)=\left\langle 1,\ 2t\right\rangle\text{.}\) Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{align*} W_2 \amp = \int_{C_2}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^1\left\langle t^2,\ 2t\right\rangle \cdot\left\langle 1,\ 2t\right\rangle dt\\ \amp = \int_0^1\left(t^2+4t^2\right)dt = \int_0^1 5t^2\,dt = \frac{5}{3}. \end{align*}
    Since \(W_1=\dfrac{3}{2}\) and \(W_2=\dfrac{5}{3}\) are not equal, the line integral of \(\mathbf{F}\) from \(O\) to \(P\) depends on the path taken, and not on the endpoints alone. Knowing where a curve starts and ends is therefore not enough, in general, to determine the work. Deciding which fields are the exception—those for which the endpoints are enough—is the question taken up in the next section, Path Independence, Conservative Fields, and Potential Functions.

    Subsection 1.4.4 Concept Check

    Checkpoint 1.4.15. Positive, negative, or zero?

    In Figure 1.4.16, determine whether the work done by the force field along the two paths \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) is positive, negative, or zero.
    🔗
    Answer.
    Zero along both paths—but for two different reasons.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The field is \(\mathbf{F}=\left\langle x,\ y\right\rangle\text{,}\) which at every point aims straight away from the origin.
    🔗
    The circle \(C_2\text{.}\) A circle centred at the origin keeps a constant distance from it, so the direction of travel is everywhere perpendicular to the outward direction: \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}=0\) at every point of the curve. The integrand of (1.4.6) vanishes identically, so the work is zero. Reversing the orientation changes nothing—zero either way.
    🔗
    The ellipse \(C_1\text{.}\) Here the integrand does not vanish: on the stretches where the curve moves away from the origin the field pushes it along and \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\gt 0\text{,}\) while on the stretches where it moves back inward \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\lt 0\text{.}\) Over one full circuit the curve returns to where it started, so the outward stretches and the inward stretches cancel exactly and the total is again zero. Reversing the orientation swaps which stretches are positive, and the total is still zero.

    Checkpoint 1.4.19. A force at a fixed angle to the path.

    An object moves along a circle of radius \(R\) centred at \(O\) and is acted upon by a force \(\mathbf{F}\) of constant magnitude \(F=\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert\text{.}\) At every moment the force makes a \(30^\circ\) angle with the unit tangent vector \(\mathbf{T}\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 1.4.20. Calculate the work done by \(\mathbf{F}\) as the object moves along the half circle \(C\) from \(A\) to \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(W=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\pi RF\approx 2.72\,RF\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We place the centre \(O\) at the origin, so that \(A=(-R,0)\) and \(B=(R,0)\text{.}\) The object goes over the top from \(A\) to \(B\text{,}\) that is, clockwise, and a parametrization of \(C\) is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t)=\left\langle -R\cos t,\ R\sin t\right\rangle, \qquad 0\le t\le\pi, \end{equation*}
    since \(\mathbf{r}(0)=\left\langle -R,0\right\rangle\) is \(A\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{r}(\pi/2)=\left\langle 0,R\right\rangle\) is the top of the circle, and \(\mathbf{r}(\pi)=\left\langle R,0\right\rangle\) is \(B\text{.}\) Its velocity, speed and unit tangent vector are
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}(t)=\left\langle R\sin t,\ R\cos t\right\rangle, \qquad \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert=R, \qquad \mathbf{T}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} =\left\langle\sin t,\ \cos t\right\rangle, \end{equation*}
    so \(ds=\left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt=R\,dt\text{.}\) At \(A\) the tangent is \(\mathbf{T}=\left\langle 0,1\right\rangle\text{,}\) straight up, as in Figure 1.4.20.
    🔗
    Using \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds\text{.}\) The form (1.4.6) is the natural one here, because we are told the angle between \(\mathbf{F}\) and \(\mathbf{T}\) rather than the force itself. By the dot-product formula,
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T} =\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert \left\lVert\mathbf{T}\right\rVert\cos(30^\circ) =F\cos(30^\circ)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,F \end{equation*}
    at every point of \(C\text{.}\) The integrand is constant, so it comes out of the integral, and what is left is the length of the half circle:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} W \amp =\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds =\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,F\int_C ds\\ \amp =\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,F\int_0^{\pi}R\,dt =\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\pi RF\approx 2.72\,RF. \end{align*}
    Check with the parametrized form. To use (1.4.9) we need \(\mathbf{F}\) itself along \(C\text{.}\) Let \(\mathbf{u}=\dfrac{\mathbf{r}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert} =\left\langle -\cos t,\ \sin t\right\rangle\) be the outward unit vector along the radius; it is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{T}\text{,}\) since \(\mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{u}=-\sin t\cos t+\cos t\sin t=0\text{.}\) The force leans \(30^\circ\) away from \(\mathbf{T}\) toward the outside of the circle (Figure 1.4.21), so
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t)\bigr) \amp =F\bigl(\cos(30^\circ)\,\mathbf{T} +\sin(30^\circ)\,\mathbf{u}\bigr)\\ \amp =F\left\langle \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\sin t-\tfrac{1}{2}\cos t,\ \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cos t+\tfrac{1}{2}\sin t\right\rangle. \end{align*}
    Dotting with the velocity, the mixed terms cancel and \(\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t=1\) does the rest:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v} =FR\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\sin^2 t-\tfrac{1}{2}\sin t\cos t +\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cos^2 t+\tfrac{1}{2}\sin t\cos t\right) =\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,FR, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} W=\int_0^{\pi}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,FR\,dt =\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\pi RF, \end{equation*}
    as before.
    🔗
    The radial part \(F\sin(30^\circ)\,\mathbf{u}\) is perpendicular to the motion and does no work; only the tangential component counts. Had the force leaned toward the centre instead, the sign of the \(\mathbf{u}\) term would flip and the work would be the same. The work is positive because the angle between \(\mathbf{F}\) and \(\mathbf{T}\) is acute, and it grows with the length of the path: around the whole circle the same argument gives \(\sqrt{3}\,\pi RF\neq 0\text{.}\) Unlike the radial field of Checkpoint 1.4.15, this force keeps pushing the object along its way, so nothing cancels when the object comes back to where it started.

    Checkpoint 1.4.22. Pulling a pendulum aside.

    A simple pendulum consists of a small bob of mass \(m\) hanging from a cord of length \(\ell\) whose mass is negligible. A horizontal force \(\mathbf{F}=F\,\mathbf{i}\) moves the bob so slowly that its acceleration is essentially zero; its magnitude \(F=\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert\) must therefore change with the angle \(\theta\) that the cord makes with the vertical. Two other forces act on the bob: gravity, \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{G}}=m\mathbf{g}\text{,}\) and the force \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{T}}\) that the cord exerts on it. See Figure 1.4.23.
    🔗
    (a)
    Calculate the work done by \(\mathbf{F}\) in moving the pendulum from \(\theta=0\) to \(\theta=\theta_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(W_{\mathbf{F}}=mg\ell\left(1-\cos\theta_0\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The path. We put the origin at the lowest position \(A\) of the bob, with \(x\) to the right and \(y\) up, so the pivot is at \((0,\ell)\text{.}\) When the cord makes the angle \(\theta\) with the vertical, the bob is \(\ell\sin\theta\) to the right of the vertical line through the pivot and \(\ell\cos\theta\) below the pivot, so its height above \(A\) is \(y=\ell-\ell\cos\theta\text{;}\) see Figure 1.4.24. We use the angle \(\theta\) itself as the parameter, in the role of \(t\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(\theta) =\left\langle \ell\sin\theta,\ \ell-\ell\cos\theta\right\rangle, \qquad 0\le\theta\le\theta_0, \end{equation*}
    from \(A=\mathbf{r}(0)=(0,0)\) to \(B=\mathbf{r}(\theta_0)\text{.}\) The velocity is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v}(\theta)=\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{d\theta} =\left\langle \ell\cos\theta,\ \ell\sin\theta\right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The forces. Write \(F_{\mathrm{T}}\) for the magnitude \(\left\lVert\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{T}}\right\rVert\text{.}\) The force of the cord points along the cord from the bob toward the pivot, so
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}=\left\langle F,\ 0\right\rangle,\qquad \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{G}}=\left\langle 0,\ -mg\right\rangle,\qquad \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{T}}=\left\langle -F_{\mathrm{T}}\sin\theta,\ F_{\mathrm{T}}\cos\theta\right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    The acceleration is essentially zero, so the three forces balance at every instant, \(\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{G}}+\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{T}} =\mathbf{0}\text{,}\) which in components reads
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \mathbf{i}:\quad \amp F-F_{\mathrm{T}}\sin\theta=0,\\ \mathbf{j}:\quad \amp F_{\mathrm{T}}\cos\theta-mg=0. \end{align*}
    Hence \(F_{\mathrm{T}}=\dfrac{mg}{\cos\theta}\) and \(F=mg\tan\theta\text{,}\) and along \(C\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(\theta)\bigr) =\left\langle mg\tan\theta,\ 0\right\rangle,\qquad \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{T}}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(\theta)\bigr) =\left\langle -mg\tan\theta,\ mg\right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    In terms of position, \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{x}{\ell-y}\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) =\left\langle \dfrac{mg\,x}{\ell-y},\ 0\right\rangle\) is a genuine force field; we only ever need it along \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The work. By (1.4.9),
    🔗
    \begin{align*} W_{\mathbf{F}} \amp =\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =\int_0^{\theta_0}\left\langle mg\tan\theta,\ 0\right\rangle \cdot\left\langle \ell\cos\theta,\ \ell\sin\theta\right\rangle d\theta\\ \amp =mg\ell\int_0^{\theta_0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\, \cos\theta\,d\theta =mg\ell\int_0^{\theta_0}\sin\theta\,d\theta\\ \amp =mg\ell\Bigl[-\cos\theta\Bigr]_0^{\theta_0} =mg\ell\left(1-\cos\theta_0\right). \end{align*}
    In the differential form (1.4.10) this is the same computation: \(M=mg\tan\theta\text{,}\) \(N=0\) and \(dx=\ell\cos\theta\,d\theta\text{,}\) so \(W_{\mathbf{F}}=\int_C M\,dx =\int_0^{\theta_0}mg\ell\sin\theta\,d\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    Calculate the work done by the gravitational force \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{G}}\) and the work done by the force \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{T}}\) of the cord over the same motion.
    🔗
    Answer.
    \(W_{\mathrm{G}}=-mg\ell\left(1-\cos\theta_0\right)\) and \(W_{\mathrm{T}}=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We use the parametrization and the forces found in part (a).
    🔗
    Gravity. The force \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{G}}=\left\langle 0,\ -mg\right\rangle\) is constant, and
    🔗
    \begin{align*} W_{\mathrm{G}} \amp =\int_C\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{G}}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =\int_0^{\theta_0}\left\langle 0,\ -mg\right\rangle \cdot\left\langle \ell\cos\theta,\ \ell\sin\theta\right\rangle d\theta\\ \amp =-mg\ell\int_0^{\theta_0}\sin\theta\,d\theta =-mg\ell\left(1-\cos\theta_0\right). \end{align*}
    The answer is \(-mg\) times the height \(\ell\left(1-\cos\theta_0\right)\) gained by the bob, and the shape of the path played no role beyond that. Gravity is one of the fields, studied in the next section, whose line integrals depend only on the endpoints.
    🔗
    The cord. Here the integrand vanishes:
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{T}}\cdot\mathbf{v} =\frac{mg}{\cos\theta} \left\langle -\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta\right\rangle \cdot\left\langle \ell\cos\theta,\ \ell\sin\theta\right\rangle =\frac{mg\ell}{\cos\theta} \left(-\sin\theta\cos\theta+\cos\theta\sin\theta\right)=0, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} W_{\mathrm{T}}=\int_C\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{T}}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =\int_0^{\theta_0}0\,d\theta=0. \end{equation*}
    In the form (1.4.6) this is immediate: the cord lies along a radius of the circular arc \(C\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{T}}\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{T}\) at every point (Figure 1.4.24), even though its magnitude \(mg/\cos\theta\) keeps changing.
    🔗
    A check. \(W_{\mathbf{F}}+W_{\mathrm{G}}+W_{\mathrm{T}} =mg\ell\left(1-\cos\theta_0\right)-mg\ell\left(1-\cos\theta_0\right)+0 =0\text{,}\) as it must be: the net force is the zero vector at every point of \(C\text{,}\) so its line integral is zero.
